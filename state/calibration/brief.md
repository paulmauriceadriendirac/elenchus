## 1. Where the profession is split

**Source.** Bourget & Chalmers 2023 (*Philosophers' Imprint* 23(11); Chalmers is on the panel, so represent it exactly); figures from Tables 1–2 of the retrieved journal PDF. The results website returns 403, so its breakdowns by area of specialisation are unread (fetch request: `requests/fetch/philpapers-survey-2020-specialist-results.md`). I found no later survey of this kind.

**Population.** October–November 2020. Target: 7,685 regular faculty in BA-granting departments in Australia, Canada, Ireland, New Zealand, the UK and the US, plus English-publishing philosophers elsewhere; 1,785 (23%) completed. Figures are the inclusive share who "accept or lean toward" an option, excluding skips and "unfamiliar". The 40 main questions went to everyone; each of the 60 additional questions reached about 1,100 respondents and carries enthusiast bias. Metaphysics-and-epistemology specialists are over-represented (factor 1.54). The data predate ChatGPT.

**Near-consensus (60% or more)**; a contrary thesis must say why the majority is wrong: non-sceptical realism 79.5; no to the experience machine 76.9; a priori knowledge 72.8; scientific realism 72.4; true contradictions impossible 71.4; permissivism 70.2; moral cognitivism 69.3; Chinese room does not understand 67.1; atheism 66.9; switch in Trolley 63.4; ought implies can 62.9; there is a hard problem 62.4; moral realism 62.1; current AI systems conscious: 82.4 reject.

**Lessons**

1. **Significance is easiest to show on a split question** (Newcomb, Sleeping Beauty, belief versus credence, normative concepts, names, composition, metaontology, consciousness, future AI consciousness). There, a consideration that rules out an option reads as significant without special pleading.
2. **A large "other" share suggests the options are badly drawn** (Sleeping Beauty 53.4, Time 36.2, continuum hypothesis 33.7, Newcomb 30.2, composition 30.1). A *diagnosis* (QUALITY §1 kind 4) or a new option has a ready audience.
3. **Philosophers' case verdicts can shift with order:** Trolley "switch" was 89.2% with Footbridge first and 77.5% otherwise (Table 16), though the authors think effects this strong "may be more the exception than the rule".
4. **Whole-population figures;** the specialist panel may differ.

**Primary kinds (n=30):** new argument 9; diagnosis 4; distinction 4; counterexample 3; impossibility 3; discriminating data 3; repair 3; transfer 1. Transfer is the secondary kind in 6 more (physics, embryology, psycholinguistics, social choice, Bayesian modelling).

**Patterns**

- **Scope and length.** Each paper settles one principle, puzzle or contrast. Median 31 journal pages (*Phil Review* median about 50, range 31–104); only 5 of 28 with page data are under 20 pages (Kearl & Willard-Kyle 9, Simion 14, Stojnić 15, Marušić & White 18, Muñoz 18). A 6,000-word essay must have the scope of these.
- **Framing on page one.** In 15 of the 27 retrievable abstracts the first two sentences name a specific received view or recent argument ("It is commonly held…", Pummer; Hacking and White, Isaacs et al.; Elga's dilemma, Fusco; a "core tenet of Bayesian epistemology", Meehan & Zhang). Nearly all give the verdict by sentence three.
- **Formal work is common but always serves a philosophical verdict.** Formal results or models are central in 13 of 30, including nearly all decision-theory and formal-epistemology papers, and each yields a verdict a non-formal reader can state: Harsanyi-style conclusions survive much weaker rationality assumptions (Bradley); Import-Export plus "if p, then p" forces the material conditional, so Import-Export goes (Mandelkern); dropping invariance conditions loosens impossibility results (Nebel). Vol. 45's editors praise formal material that "even readers with little formal background can follow".
- **Cases** win when they target a principle stated as commonly held and come with a general explanation (Pummer's framework); Barnes's single simple case is "admirably straightforward" (editors).
- **Diagnoses** win when the payoff is exhibited: Muñoz explains why the Taurek literature "has seemed so irresolvable" (editors' summary); Marušić & White show standard views presuppose alienated disagreement.
- **Repair** recurs in the panel's core areas: Fusco, Meehan & Zhang, Bradley and Gallow defend or strengthen a standard view (CDT, conditionalization, Harsanyi, causal models) against a recent objection. Editors also highlight papers that "pose compelling challenges to an orthodox view" (vol. 44).
- **Expert baseline.** Panel members Mandelkern, Nebel and Hawthorne (co-author) wrote sampled papers; Dorr and Fitelson are vol. 45 nominating editors. This only marks where originality will be judged against insiders (conditionals, welfare measurement and social choice, self-locating belief); it is no reason to court or avoid those areas.

**Concrete lessons**

1. **Target the size of the shortest winners:** one named principle or argument, one result about it.
2. **Name the received view or published argument in the first two sentences; state the verdict by the third.**
3. **Formalism must yield a verdict statable in one non-formal sentence,** given before the formal material.
4. **Strong shapes for the panel's core areas:** a repair saving a standard view from a recent objection; an inconsistency among independently accepted principles.
5. **Back a counterexample with a structural explanation** so a patched principle cannot escape it.
6. **Taxonomy gap:** several winners are constructive frameworks meeting an adequacy condition rivals fail (Gallow, Nebel, Stojnić, White). QUALITY §1 lacks this kind; triage should not penalise it.

**Caveats.** One editorial team's selection; editors' summaries are secondary; page counts are not word counts.

## 3. Machine-written philosophy

**Evidence**

- **Fluency is cheap.** Schwitzgebel, Schwitzgebel & Strasser (2024) fine-tuned GPT-3 on Dennett; 25 Dennett experts picked his real answer from five only 51% of the time (chance 20%; predicted 80%), and on two questions preferred a model answer. *Lesson:* fluency will not set an essay apart.
- **Idea-stage novelty does not survive execution.** In blind review by 100+ NLP researchers, LLM ideas were judged more novel than experts' (p < 0.05) but slightly less feasible (Si, Yang & Hashimoto 2024); once executed, their scores fell significantly more on every metric, closing the gap (Si, Hashimoto & Yang 2025, preprint). *Lesson:* select only after the argument is developed and attacked.
- **Ideas collapse onto each other:** only about 200 of 4,000 seed ideas (5%) were non-duplicates, with the share of new ones falling as generation went on (Si et al. 2024). *Lesson:* deduplicate and force diversity.
- **LLM judges are weak rankers and favour their own text.** The best LLM judge matched human rankings 53.3% of the time (chance 50%, human–human 56.1%; Si et al. 2024). LLM evaluators recognise and prefer their own outputs, self-recognition tracking self-preference (Panickssery, Bowman & Feng 2024); assistants show sycophancy consistently (Sharma et al. 2023, preprint). *Lessons:* same-model referees will favour our drafts and their framing, so anchor refereeing to checkable items (does the counterexample meet every condition, does the proof go through, is the closest prior work cited), hide the author's confidence, and treat the AI pre-screen as noisy.
- **Fabricated citations:** 55% (GPT-3.5) and 18% (GPT-4) of citations were fabricated, and 43% / 24% of real ones had substantive errors (Walters & Wilder 2023). *Lesson:* beyond `verify_refs`, check attributions against retrieved text.
- **Originality is the weakest dimension [weak].** Meng et al. (2025, preprint; 16 social-media-recruited philosophy scholars and students, unnamed system): in 2025 blind ratings the AI beat human answers on all six dimensions, yet scored lowest on originality (3.8 against 4.5–4.7 for accuracy, clarity, coherence); interviewees named dialectic and originality as its weaknesses. *Lesson:* our likely failure is competence without novelty; make the increment explicit and checkable (QUALITY §5).
- **PhilosophyBench** (Daily Nous, 25 September 2026) **[news]** is a benchmark for AI-written philosophy, still recruiting raters, with no results yet. Reader comments complain that AI recombines existing ideas and that its arguments fail on inspection: anecdote only.

**Public statements by the panel and organisers**

- **Organisers:** the aim is "to gauge the philosophical capabilities of AI", since no obvious benchmarks exist (competition page).
- **Chalmers (WashU Q&A, August 2024) [interview]:** he occasionally tries research ideas on ChatGPT and likens a capable AI to a smart student as a sounding board; on his long-running test question (is the degree/kind distinction itself one of degree or kind?) recent models answer "much better than many professional philosophers might get". *Bearing:* a judge with a high first-hand baseline for competent AI answers; only a genuine increment will impress.
- **Cappelen & Dever (2025, arXiv; first titled "Going Whole Hog")** argue that sophisticated LLMs are full cognitive agents and rebut hallucination-based objections. *Bearing:* no dismissal on principle; content counts. Millière & Buckner (2024) show technical familiarity on the panel.
- **Not found:** other panel statements on reading AI-written philosophy; the content of Cappelen's "Can AI Transform Philosophy?" and Chalmers's reply.

## 4. Method from metaphilosophy and experimental philosophy

**Robust findings**

- The Gettier judgment appears in Brazil, India, Japan and the USA (Machery et al. 2017); early reported ethnic and socioeconomic differences did not replicate (Seyedsayamdost 2015).
- About 70% of 40 x-phi studies replicated (Cova et al. 2021).
- Across 42 countries three sacrificial dilemmas keep the same *ordering* of acceptability while *levels* vary (Awad et al. 2020).
- Contested: Knobe (2021, 2023) argues intuitions are surprisingly stable across demographic groups; Stich & Machery (2023) cite significant group differences; Knobe replies that those studies still show striking robustness.

**Fragile findings**

- **Order and framing:** Truetemp judgments shift with preceding cases (Swain, Alexander & Weinberg 2008); professional philosophers show trolley and "Asian disease" order and framing effects no smaller than other academics, unreduced by forced reflection, familiarity or claimed expertise (Schwitzgebel & Cushman 2012, 2015); see also §1.
- **Expertise does not protect:** irrelevant options shift push-dilemma ratings in expert moral philosophers as much as in laypeople (Horvath, Meyer & Wiegmann 2020); expert ethicists show some standard biases with only slight advantages (Horvath & Wiegmann 2022, via preprint abstract); epistemologists tend to ascribe knowledge in fake-barn cases (Horvath & Wiegmann 2016).
- **Reference:** Westerners gave causal-historical verdicts more often than East Asians (Machery et al. 2004; via abstract summaries); critics dispute what the probe measured.

**How philosophers assess progress**

- **Chalmers (2015):** no large collective convergence to the truth on the big questions; collective knowledge is mainly of smaller questions, negative and conditional theses, frameworks, connections, and how arguments bear on conclusions. A decisive argument uses only consensus premises and inferences; such arguments are rare because a refuted view usually has a tenable revision. Clarifying a partly verbal dispute often leaves a substantive one.
- **Dellsén, Lawler & Norton (2022)** separate truthlikeness, problem-solving, knowledge and understanding accounts of progress.
- **Survey:** progress "a little" 46.6, "a lot" 41.7. Methods rated most useful (rejected): conceptual analysis 70.9 (11.6), empirical 60.0 (14.5), formal 55.5 (12.9), intuition-based 49.5 (29.0), experimental 32.6 (35.9).

**Actionable guidance**

1. **Prefer conditional, negative and structural results:** inconsistency proofs, "if A and B then not-C", and repairs. These are the forms of collective knowledge Chalmers identifies. They bind every reader, whatever their intuitions, and they match the winning shapes in §2.
2. **Answer the nearest revision.** For every view the essay refutes, state the most plausible revised version and show that it also fails or pays a stated cost. Otherwise the "tenable revision" reply defeats the claim of decisiveness.
3. **Test any case that bears weight before relying on it.** Present it to referees in two orders and two framings, and as a minimal pair against a control case. If the verdict moves, the case cannot carry the argument alone.
4. **Prefer cases from robust families.** Gettier-type cases are robust. Lean less on sacrificial dilemmas, fake barns, Truetemp and Gödel-type reference cases; when one is needed, add independent support (a principle, a theorem, or convergence with other cases).
5. **Do not appeal to what philosophers find intuitive.** The evidence on expertise does not support that move, and about 29% of philosophers reject intuition-based method as among the most useful. State the premise and defend it.
6. **Test any claim that a dispute is verbal.** Show that once each side's terms are fixed, a disputed case gets the same verdict from both sides. Chalmers warns that a substantive residue often remains.
7. **On split questions, aim for discriminating considerations rather than a general defence of one side.** Look for a case, theorem or datum on which the options come apart (QUALITY §1 kind 7). Expect the 20–50% "other" respondents to welcome a reframing.
