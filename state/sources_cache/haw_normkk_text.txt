Abstract
In their 2016 ‘Taking a Chance on KK’, Jeremy Goodman and Bernhard Salow offer 
an interesting epistemic model that validates KK. The springboard for my discus-
sion will be a mistake in their presentation, one that points to a real problem with 
the model. My remarks will have a broad interest, since certain of the key examples 
point to intriguing questions concerning the epistemic contours of testimony and 
memory. I’ll also raise instructive difficulties for a theoretically important weaken-
ing of the JTB treatment of knowledge.
1 Introduction
In their 2016 ‘Taking a Chance on KK’, Jeremy Goodman and Bernhard Salow offer 
an interesting epistemic model that validates KK. The springboard for my discus-
sion will be a mistake in their presentation, one that points to a real problem with 
the model. My remarks will have a broad interest, since certain of the key examples 
point to intriguing questions concerning the epistemic contours of testimony and 
memory. I’ll also raise instructive difficulties for a theoretically important weaken-
ing of the JTB treatment of knowledge.
Here is how the 2016 Goodman and Salow model works. (I will keep things some-
what informal, providing just enough detail for the purposes of this brief essay.) States 
of the world (hereafter, ‘worlds’) are ordered by two transitive relations, a relation of 
being at least as normal as and a relation of being way more normal than, with the 
obvious connections that these labels suggest. (Note that each relation is supposed to 
be non-contingent.) Justification works like this: Each agent has a body of evidence, 
and that evidence rules out some worlds. The ones that remain are the ‘evidentially 
accessible worlds’. One is justified in ruling out all and only those worlds that are 
either not evidentially accessible or which are way less normal than some evidentially 
accessible world. Call the worlds that remain the ‘justification accessible worlds’. (In

accessible worlds that are also justification accessible). The worlds that, for all one 
knows, one is in—the ‘epistemically accessible worlds’ are (i) the justification acces-
sible worlds, (ii) the actual world and (iii) all the worlds in between the actual world 
and the justification accessible worlds (i.e. all the worlds that are neither justifica-
tion accessible nor identical to the actual world, but which are at least as normal as 
the actual world. Of course, in certain cases, no extra worlds are added in by clause 
(iii).) One knows P just in case P is true at all the epistemically accessible worlds. 
Given this model, in any case where some proposition P is true at all the epistemi-
cally accessible worlds, the proposition that one knows P will be true at all the epis-
temically accessible worlds too. If P is the strongest proposition known at the actual 
world, then insofar as the actual world is not justification accessible, P will not be the 
strongest proposition known at certain epistemically accessible worlds. But P will be 
known at all the epistemically accessible worlds. Meanwhile, if the actual world is 
one of the justification accessible worlds, the strongest proposition known will be the 
strongest proposition known at all the epistemically accessible worlds, namely that 
which is true at all and only the justification accessible worlds. 1 The model involves 
various idealizations—for example, it doesn’t worry about the distinction between 
knowing and being in a position to know. But it is nevertheless intended as an illumi-
nating model of how knowledge works.
2  Coin Flipping
Goodman and Salow (2016) apply the KK friendly model to a set-up discussed in 
Dorr et al. (2014). One thousand fair coins are laid out in sequence, and a coin flip-
per will flip each in turn until a heads comes up or else all have been flipped. Dorr, 
Goodman and Hawthorne show that on very natural assumptions (including, nota-
bly, the anti-sceptical assumption that one knows the set-up and also knows that not 
all 1000 coins will be flipped), the following principle is false: 
Fair Coins
If you know a coin is fair and for all you know it will be flipped, then for all 
you know it will land tails.

The argument goes like this: Given the anti-sceptical assumption, there is a first 
coin—Cn + 1—that you know won’t be flipped. (That coin isn’t the first coin in the 
sequence, which you know will be flipped). The coin before Cn + 1—i.e. Cn—is 
a coin which for all you know will be flipped: but you know Cn won’t be flipped 
and land tails, since that would mean Cn + 1 is flipped—something you know won’t 
happen.
Dorr et al. (2014) suggest that there is a true principle in the vicinity, namely:
Weak Fair Coins: If a coin is fair and will be flipped, then for all you know it 
will land tails.2
It is easy enough to write down a toy model of knowledge with “margins” that vali-
dates Weak Fair Coins but not Fair Coins: Suppose for example, that if the number 
of tails flipped is x, the strongest thing you know is that there won’t be x plus 20 tails 
in a row. Then anti-scepticism will be true in all but those very unusual “sceptical 
scenarios” where there are at least 980 tails flipped in a row. And in the non-scep-
tical scenarios, there will, in this model, be counterexamples to Fair Coins but not 
Weak Fair Coins.
But as Goodman and Salow (2016) point out (following Dorr et  al., 2014, 
Sect. 7), Weak Fair Coins is not a happy fallback for the KK lover. For if Weak Fair 
Coins is true, it seems like the sort of thing that can be known. But assuming clo-
sure for knowledge, it is problematic to assume KK and also that Weak Fair Coins 
is known. Suppose one knows 100 won’t be flipped. By closure (and knowledge of 
the set up) one knows that coin 99 won’t both be flipped and come up tails. By KK 
one knows that one knows this. By Weak Fair coins and closure one then knows that 
coin 99 won’t both be flipped at all, since Weak Fair Coins says that if 99 is flipped, 
one doesn’t know that it won’t be flipped and come up tails. The reasoning can be 
repeated to show, for each coin less than 100, one knows that it won’t be flipped, in 
violation of factivity for knowledge. They thus have to allow for situations in which, 
say, exactly 99 coins are flipped and one knows in advance of the coin flipper doing 
their thing that the coin flipper will not flip 100 coins. Moreoever, if one has such 
knowledge and does not pay attention to the coin flipper—perhaps one goes to the 
pub instead – it seems natural to think that one retains that knowledge. (See Good-
man and Salow’s discussion of “Settledness”). Assuming this, we also get a counter-
example to.
Very Weak Fair Coins
If a coin is about to be flipped then for all one knows it will land tails.

Extremely Weak Fair Coins
If a coin is fair and you know it is about to be flipped, then for all you know it 
will land tails.3
They then offer a natural way of applying the KK normality model to the case 
of the coin flipper. Suppose the normality ordering works so that for each value of 
n between 1 and 999 inclusive, a world with exactly n + 1 tails is less normal than 
a world with n tails, where worlds that differ by one in the number of tails are such 
that neither is way more normal than the other. Suppose further that a world w1 is 
way more normal than another w2 if there is never in w1 a substantial chance (for 
some suitable interpretation of ‘substantial’) of the sequence being as long as in w2.
Consider two people, Jack and Jill, in a world w where there are 30 tails in a 
row followed by coin 31 being flipped and coming up heads. Suppose further that 
Jack and Jill know the coin flipping protocol. The justification accessible worlds will 
involve quite a few less heads than 30 (for natural choices of ‘substantial’). Before 
things get going, Jack and Jill’s epistemically accessible worlds consist of the jus-
tification accessible worlds, world w, and all the worlds in between. Since a 31 tail 
world is less normal than w, it will not be epistemically accessible and so, while 
coin 31 is flipped in w, Jack and Jill know at this point—according to this model—
that coin 31 won’t be both flipped and come up tails (a counterexample to Weak 
Fair Coins). Jack watches the flipper, Jill goes to the pub. By the time the flipper has 
flipped 30 tails, Jill has received no new relevant evidence and still knows that she 
is not in a world where coin 31 is flipped and comes up tails. While Jill is a counter-
example to Very Weak Fair Coins, she is not a counterexample to Extremely Weak 
Fair coins, since she doesn’t know coin 31 is about to be flipped. Consider Jack, 
meanwhile, who watches the coins getting flipped, getting more and more evidence 
about outcomes. By the time he has seen 30 tails come up, the top ranked eviden-
tially accessible world will be one where exactly 30 tails come up. But since that 
world is not way more normal than a world where exactly 31 tails come up, the 31 
tails world will be justification accessible. And since the epistemically accessible 
worlds have to include the justification accessible ones, Jack will no longer know 
that there will not be 31 tails in a row: Jack’s initial knowledge is defeated and so 
Jack is not a counterexample to Extremely Weak Fair Coins either. That said, Good-
man and Salow’s models do not succeed in offering anything like a general vindica-
tion of Extremely Weak Fair Coins. Indeed it is not hard to model counterexamples 
to it. Consider the following setup:
Jack picks a coin from a bucket. A coin flipper will flip that coin and carry on 
flipping it until heads comes up or until the coin has been flipped 1000 times. The 
coins in the bucket are of two sorts (with no overt tell tale signs as to which sort a 
coin is). Half the coins are fair coins. Half the coins are radio-controlled in such a 
way that they will come up tails for the first 30 flips and heads after that. Jack in

3  Vandalism
There are various ways to try to tinker. For those that want to do so, it might be 
helpful to look at a case which is a little less “cheesy” than one involving radio-
controlled coins but where similar themes are at work. Suppose there is an elec-
tion in Z-land involving two parties, X and Y. A certain locale is pretty much split 
between X and Y supporters. A political vandal concocts a plan along the follow -
ing lines: “I will go along the houses along the left side of the street and the first 
time I see X-friendly paraphernalia, I will egg the house (i.e. throw eggs at it) and 
then make my escape.” Jack and Jill know the set up. They know that there are 300 
houses on the left hand side of the street (it is a very long street). (I’ll be using 
the numbering system ‘1’ to ‘300’, where ‘1’ names the first house that the vandal 
encounters.) The anti-sceptical assumption that they know that a house on the street 
will get egged is very natural here. Even those who have sceptical leanings about 
coin tosses will likely be pretty happy—unless their scepticism is quite radical—to 
concede that Jack and Jill can know about the set up and also know that the vandal 
will be egging exactly one house on the left hand side of the street (and then making 
their escape).7 Suppose also that Jack and Jack have no insider knowledge about the 
occupants of particular homes on the street, only that the locale is keenly divided 
between X-supporters and Y-supporters In fact house 31 gets egged. The natural 
way to model things has it that for number n = 2 or greater, worlds get less normal

Let us continue the story. Jill goes to the pub, and retains her knowledge that one 
of the first 31 houses are going to get egged. Jack watches the vandal. At a certain 
point Jack knows the first 30 houses have not been egged. Jack’s knowledge that one 
of the first 31 houses will get egged has been defeated, but not Jill’s. Jill then calls 
Jack and tells Jack ‘One of the first 31 houses will get egged’.
Here is a version of the story that does not make trouble for the model but which 
is interesting in its own right. In this version, Jack knows that Jill’s basis for the 
speech is inductive and that she has no special insider knowledge. Here, since a 32 
egging world is accessible to Jack, though not to Jill, then assuming the relevant 
model, Jack would be speaking truly if he said “Maybe Jill knows the thing she said, 
maybe she is speaking falsely. If the next house is egged then, as a keen student of 
Goodman and Salow, I reckon that Jill will have spoken from knowledge. But even 
with her having spoken, and even if I believe her, the world where 31 is not egged 
and 32 is egged is justification accessible and so I am not in a position to know that 
what Jill said is true.” So in this version of the story, the model gives us an interest-
ing transmission failure.8 Even if Jack trusts Jill and Jill knows, Jack does not get to 
know. And that’s not a bad result for Goodman and Salow—otherwise there would 
be a really easy counterexample to Extremely Weak Vandalism.

But here is a version of the story that is far more problematic. Suppose that Jack 
knows Jill is being honest, saying what she believes, but does not know whether Jill 
is speaking on the basis of induction or insider knowledge. Further Jack knows, inter 
alia, that if she is not merely relying on induction she is speaking the truth. And sup-
pose it would, relative to Jack’s evidence, be pretty weird for Jill to be making that 
speech on inductive grounds. (Indeed this is a very natural assumption: It IS pretty 
weird for people to make speeches like that on inductive grounds.) And suppose as 
before that Jack has seen 30 houses not be egged. Here it is natural to think Jack is 
justified in thinking Jill is speaking from insider information. So the world of the 
story does not belong to the zone of justification accessible worlds. And extrapolating 
from the above discussion, it is natural to think that the world where the vandal eggs 
31 and Jill speaks on an inductive basis is more normal than the world where the van-
dal eggs 32 and Jill speaks on that basis. So all the worlds in the justification zone are 
worlds where one of the first 31 get egged, and the world Jack is in is a world where 
one of the first 31 get egged. And all the worlds where Jill is speaking from induction 
and it is not the case where one of the first 31 get egged are worlds less normal than 
the world of the story, since the later the egging the less normal the world is. So all 
the epistemically accessible worlds are ones where house 31 is egged.
In this version of the story, knowledge is transmitted! Without any special insider 
information (after all Jill was just using inductive resources) Jack gets to know 
house 31 will get egged and also knows the vandal is about to investigate 31. This is 
a troubling result for the model. As before there is a variant principle that isn’t coun-
terexampled, in this case one that makes ‘has no insider knowledge’ narrow scope. 
Since for all Jack knows he has insider knowledge from Jill, the strengthened prin-
ciple survives. But Extremely Weak Vandalism seems compelling and it fails on the 
KK model. Note that we can also get a structurally similar (though slightly more far 
fetched) testimonial counterexample to Extremely Weak Fair Coins if we can fill out 
the story in such a way that Jack is justified in thinking that Jill has special clairvoy-
ant powers. (I’ll leave it as an exercise for the reader to fill in the details.)9
4  Testimony and Memory

Jack is justified in believing that the testimonial basis is something other than 
it is. How should we theorize about this kind of case? Note that one particularly

Alleged Misprint
Jones reads in the Orange County Gazette that the New England Patriots won

How should we theorize about such cases? (I can report that many informants had 
no clear judgment.) There are a range of potentially relevant principles that I suspect 
some philosophers would be initially tempted by, but which, on further reflection, 
are quite dodgy. For example here is one:
Attempt One
 If a testifier who knows P tells you that P, and you trust the testifier, but your basis 
for trust is that you justifiedly think that the testifier is using one method, when in 
fact they are using another, then you don’t get to know that P by trusting the testifier.
This doesn’t seem quite right. Suppose I trust Jones about the Manchester City 
score, justifiedly believing that Jones has gotten the score from a newspaper. In fact 
Jones came to know the score by attending the game. I really don’t think it is plausi-
ble that knowledge fails to be transmitted.
Attempt Two
 If a testifer who knows P tells you that P, and you trust the testifier, but you would 
not trust the testifier if you knew which method the testifer was using, then you don’t 
get to know that P.
I am not convinced. Suppose that I believe that no one can know that it is Janet on 
the top of the hill from their vantage point by investigating the matter with the naked 
eye. In fact Smith has extremely acute eyesight and came to know that it is Janet 
just by looking. Smith tells me that Janet is on top of the hill. I justifiedly think that 
Smith is relying on a previous conversation with Janet and believe Smith. Isn’t it at 
least somewhat natural to think I get to know?
Similarly suppose I think Johnson is bad at math. I am also bad at math: I 
wouldn’t understand a proof even if someone tried to explain it to me. Johnson 
tells me that 22/7 is greater than pi and I justifiedly think that Johnson has learned 
this from some learned mathematician. In fact Johnson excels at math and believes 
this on the basis of mathematical reasoning. Here it is quite natural to think I get to

Notice that similar issues to the ones I have been raising also arise for memory. 
One can forget the source of information retained in memory. In some cases you will 
have false justified beliefs about the source of memory beliefs. And we can construct 
versions of those cases which mirror various testimonial cases above. For example:
Forgotten Source11
You learn from Jones that P and come to know P. P gets stored in preservative 
memory but you forget where you got P from. You come to believe that Jones 
is a bad epistemic source on the basis of misleading information. If you knew 
that your memory belief was gotten from Jones, then you would disown it. But 
you don’t know that. Is the memory belief knowledge?
The parallelism is suggestive and may lead one to search for more abstract princi-
ples to handle both kinds of cases. Readers of the earlier sections might well think 
that one way to tinker with Goodman and Salow’s model is to institute something 
along the lines of:
Abstract Fix
One cannot come to know P if one’s path to believing P essentially relies on a 
false belief.

But you don’t prior to meeting the testifier know whether the result this time around is the same and so 
come to believe the Patriots won this time round from the testifier. The principle suggests that while in 
the natural filling out of the original Alleged Misprint case, you do not get to know, this variant is dif-

to divide cases in an unintuitive way. (The principle is also a bit tricky to apply since it is up for grabs

Such tinkering would initiate another round of Gettierology. Notice that if you share 
my (albeit tentative) judgment about the cases above, then a variant of the principle 
that just used ‘relied’ is false. My trust in each of Jones, Smith and Johnson relied on 
a false belief about their method. So the principle as stated will have to lean heavily 
on the language of ‘essential reliance’. Here is Williamson’s (2007, p. 145) gloss on 
‘essential premise’: “The principle applies only to essential premises, those that figure 
in all the inferences on which the relevant belief in the conclusion is based”.
Adapting this to the matter of reliance, the contents of the false justified beliefs 
do seem to be essential:there is no other basis for the testimonially acquired belief 
that does not involve the relevant belief about the testifier. So construed, the Abstract 
Fix is no good. (For further discussion of variant notions of ‘essential premise’, see 
Hawthorne & Rabinowitz, 2017). Moreover, as we shall see in the next section, 
there are variants of the kind of problem that I have raised that do not involve reli-
ance on a false belief at all and so the question of essential reliance on a false belief 
is neither here nor there.
For what it’s worth, I’m rather dubious that the cluster of issues raised here about 
testimony, memory and defeat will be adequately handled by a principle or two that 
is stress tested by considering a few cases in imagination, especially when so many 
of the relevant cases seem to elicit no very clear judgment. If there is a path forward, 
it is more likely to consist in epistemic modelling: we get abductive evidence for 
the worth of a model by seeing that it does very well on matters we are pretty clear 
about, and then see what it has to offer about the more murky issues about testimony 
and memory that I have raised. I hope to have convinced the reader that in this con-
nection, we must improve on Goodman and Salow’s KK model. It is of course a 
further question—one for another time—whether the more general resources of nor-
mality frameworks might provide the necessary illumination.
5  Weak JTB
Things are a little worse for the KK normality models than I have been letting on 
so far. The KK model that Goodman and Salow endorse goes in for the following 
principle:
Weak JTB
If P is the strongest proposition one justifiably believes and P is true, then one 
knows P
(I call it ‘Weak JTB’ because it is strictly logically weaker than ordinary JTB.) 
Goodman and Salow would certainly not endorse JTB. Take a case where actuality 
is not justification accessible and where there are worlds in between the actual world 
and the worlds that are justification accessible. Then one can get a counterexam-
ple to JTB by taking the proposition that is true at all and only the worlds that are

to Weak JTB in their KK model.12
Now the examples that I have been dealing with so far involve cases of justi-
fied false belief—in one case a false justified belief that the coin is radio-controlled, 
in the other that a certain person is speaking from insider knowledge. They thus 
involve cases where the world of the story is outside the domain of justification-
accessible worlds. One might hope that one can control for the problems I have been 
raising by keeping Weak JTB but tweaking the clauses for epistemic accessibility in 
cases where actuality lies outside the zone of justification accessible worlds. 13 But 
another kind of case raises a deeper problem.
Suppose that after seeing 10 tails, the strongest thing one is justified in believing

will come up heads next. (Here we assume background knowledge that the radio-

Salow normality models this is easy to model: the radio-controlled coin hypothesis 
ranks top, the fair and heads at 11 world(s) are less normal but not way less normal 
than top, and the worlds where head occurs after more than 11 flips are way less

Now we need just appeal to the Weak JTB part of the theory to make trouble for 
Extremely Weak Fair Coins: The strongest thing one is justified in believing of a 
coin that is in fact fair and about to come up heads is that either the coin is radio-
controlled to come up heads next or else is fair and will come up heads next. By 
Weak JTB one knows that it is either radio-controlled and about to come up heads or 
else fair and about to come up heads. By closure one knows that it is about to come 
up heads. So we have a counterexample to Extremely Weak Fair Coins.
All this argument needs is (i) Single Premise Closure (ii) Weak JTB and (iii) situ-
ations can arise where a fair coin is about to be tossed heads and the strongest thing 
one is justified in believing is something that entails that it is either non-fair and will 
come up heads next or else is fair and will come up heads next. Since Weak JTB and 
Single Premise Closure are pretty much integral to the style of models Goodman 
and Salow are using, and since it is very hard to see how one might block possible 
cases of justification in the problematic disjunction, the problems for KK versions 
of their models look rather deep indeed. Along the way we also see why it is hope-
less to think that they can keep things under control by requiring that one’s belief 
not essentially rely on justified belief in a falsehood—after all, the strongest thing 
one is justified in believing in these variant cases is true and so one is not relying 
on a falsehood, let alone essentially relying on one. Along the way we also see that, 
normality-based or not, a KK model must either deny Weak JTB, deny Single Prem-
ise Closure, or else deny that it can happen that the strongest thing one is justified in 
believing entails a relevantly problematic disjunction.14
