A behavioural money‑pump argument for completeness
Johan E. Gustafsson1 
Accepted: 26 January 2025 / Published online: 24 May 2025 
© The Author(s) 2025
Abstract
Completeness says that, for every pair of prospects, at least one of the prospects is 
at least as preferred as the other. I present a new money-pump argument that Com-
pleteness is a requirement of rationality. In comparison with earlier money-pump 
arguments for Completeness, this argument relies on a unidimensional form of sto-
chastic dominance and the behavioural assumption that agents pick in a probabilistic 
manner when no option is optimal. Moreover, unlike some of the previous argu-
ments, the new argument is based on a forcing money pump, that is, an exploita-
tion scheme where the agent is rationally required at each step to go along with the 
scheme.

How do you select an option in a choice between options when no option is pre-
ferred to the others? A standard answer is that you don’t choose: you pick. 7 If, in a 
choice between options X and  Y, you predictably pick X by habit (or by some tie-
breaking rule), then it seems that you effectively prefer X to  Y (or, at least, regard 
X as at least as preferred as  Y).8 Instead, we shall be concerned with the case where 
your preferential gap between two options makes your behaviour uncertain (at least 
to yourself). Let’s say an agent probabilistically picks between two options if the 
agent does not know in advance what they would select if they were to face that 
choice.9
If you pick in a fully blind way, it seems that the probability that each alterna-
tive will be selected is uniform. Picking in that fully blind way makes most sense 
when your preferential attitudes for the options are exactly symmetrical. 10 Having a 
preferential gap between two options, however, doesn’t necessarily require that your 
preferential attitudes are symmetrical between the options. For instance, it’s plausi-
ble, given your preferences in (1) and (2), that your preferential comparison of A and 
B has a somewhat different balance than your preferential comparison of A and B−− . 
Plausibly, you are more likely to select A over B−− than to select A over B.
Hence we do not assume that you probabilistically pick with a probability distri-
bution that gives an equal probability for each option. We allow (but do not require) 
that these probability distributions differ for different pairs of gap-related options 
when you pick between them. (We will assume, however, that these probability dis-
tributions are precise and knowable rather than Knightian. 11 But we will cover the 
Knightian and imprecise cases later on.)
Suppose then that, in a choice between A and B, you probabilistically pick A with 
probability p (where 0 < p < 1 ). And, in a choice between A and B−− , you proba-
bilistically pick B−− with probability q (where 0 < q < 1 ). We assume that neither p 
nor q is equal to 0 or 1, because (as mentioned), if you certainly select X in a choice 
between X and Y, then you effectively prefer X to Y.

Moreover, we assume the following requirement of rationality:12
The Preferential Behaviour Restriction   For all prospects X and  Y, the agent’s 
behaviour at a choice between X and Y only depends on the agent’s preferential 
attitudes for X and Y.
The idea is that your behaviour in a choice between two options should be based 
on your preferential attitudes for those options. But note that, if you have a prefer -
ential gap between two options, the relevant preferential attitudes might be some-
thing richer than merely having a preferential gap. Still, the Preferential Behaviour 
Restriction is here taken to rule out that your choice behaviour in a choice between 
two options depends on any parts of a decision problem that can no longer be 
reached from the current choice node.13

Accordingly, this case is a money pump. We can think of the choice nodes as trad-
ing opportunities. At each choice node, you receive a trade offer, which you accept 
by going up and you turn down by going down. And we can think of you as initially 
possessing the prospect [A, r; B,1 − r] , that is, what you would get if you were to 
turn down all trade offers. At node 1, you are offered a trade from [A, r; B,1 − r] to 
[A−,r; B−,1 − r] . If you were to turn down that trade and E occurred, you would 
be offered a trade from A to B−− at node 4. And, if you were to turn down the trade 
at node 1 and E did not occur, you would be offered a trade from B to A at node 5. 
Since you will go up at node  1, you are guaranteed to pay (letting the unidimen-
sional sourings be monetary) for what you could have walked away with for free.
Note that this money pump cannot be blocked by foresight. You know the whole 
exploitation set-up in advance. And we assumed that you make your choice at 
node 1 with the help of backward induction. So, even though you have foresight and 
use backward induction, you prefer going up at node 1. And so you get exploited.17

Could this money pump be blocked with the help of resolute choice — that is, 
could it be blocked if you stick to plans even if you would not have followed those 
plans if you had not adopted them? 18 On the conservative form of resolute choice, 
resolute agents stick to a plan as long as they do not prefer to deviate from that 
plan.19 If you are this kind of agent, you could adopt a plan at node 1 to go down at 
each choice node. Thereby, you can predict at node 1 that you will go down at nodes 
4 and 5. And then, taking this prediction into account, you are rationally required to 
go down at node 1. Hence you avoid being exploited.
This resolute approach, however, violates the Preferential Behaviour Restriction. 
Since we hold your preferential attitudes fixed throughout the decision problem, you 
would still have a preferential gap between the available options at nodes 4 and 5. 
So, acting based on your preferential attitudes, you would go up at nodes 4 and 5 
with some probability. Accordingly, if you resolutely follow the plan to go down 
(with certainty) at each choice node, your behaviour at those nodes cannot solely 
depend on your preferential attitudes. It must also depend on your having adopted 
the plan to go down at each choice node. But why would you care about following 
that plan at nodes 4 and 5? What should matter is how you feel about the available 
options at those nodes. The mere fact that you initially adopted a plan to go down at 
these nodes does not seem to give you any additional reason for going down.20
Note, in addition, that, back at node 1 when you adopted the plan, you had the 
same preferential attitudes about the options at nodes 4 and 5 as you have when you 
reach those nodes. So, even at the time you adopted the plan to go down at each 
choice node, you did not prefer the outcome of going up at node 4 or 5 to the out-
come of going down. Hence, even if you defer to your past self, you don’t seem to 
have any reason to care about that plan at nodes 4 and 5.
It may be objected that the plan to walk away from all trade offers isn’t avail-
able in the relevant sense at node 1, since there is some positive probability that you 
would accept one of the offers at nodes 4 and 5. Note, however, that what makes 
the plan to walk away from all offers unavailable in this sense is your incomplete 
preferential attitudes — which is the target of the argument. 21 So your incomplete 
preferential attitudes are still to blame for your exploitability.

But what if your uncertainty about what you would select at nodes 4 and 5 is 
quantifiable but imprecise? We can model your imprecise credences as the set 
of precise probability functions that are sharpenings of your credences. And we 
adopt the following requirement of rationality:
The Strong Principle of Sharpened Unidimensional Stochastic Dominance 
If U and V  are imprecise prospects such that, on each precise probability 
function that is a sharpening of the agent’s credences, there is a probabil-
ity p and prospects X , X− , Y, such that (i) X− is a souring of X , (ii) X ≻ X− , 
(iii) 0 < p ≤ 1 , (iv) U is equivalent to prospect [X,p;Y,1 − p] , and (v) V  is 
equivalent to prospect [X−,p;Y,1 − p] , then U ≻ V.

Then, by the Strong Principle of Sharpened Unidimensional Stochastic Dominance, 
it follows that the imprecise prospect of [A, E; B, ¬E] is preferred (using backward 
induction) to the imprecise prospect of going down at node 1 — since, for each 
sharpening, we can reason as we did before in the precise-probability case. And, by 
the Unidimensional Continuity of Preference, it follows that the imprecise prospect 
of [A−, E; B−, ¬E] is preferred to the imprecise prospect of going down at node  1. 
Accordingly, you prefer the imprecise prospect of going up at node 1 to the impre-
cise prospect of going down at that node. So you would still get exploited.

ment still works.
