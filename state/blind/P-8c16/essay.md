# Learning Nothing New: What the Total-Accuracy Argument for Conditionalization Rests On

**Abstract.** Meehan and Zhang argue that conditionalization is the rational updating rule even when evidence is opaque. Among globally evidentially constant rules, they argue, conditionalization has the highest total expected accuracy across updating problems. They show that the argument succeeds exactly when the weighting of updating problems is extensional, and they suggest both that extensionality is close to explanatory bedrock and that it is closely connected to evidence internalism. I prove that extensionality is neither disguised internalism nor bedrock. Their uniform total ranks such rules exactly as a total over transparent yes/no questions does, so their main theorem is the Greaves–Wallace theorem summed. But the conclusion survives when only opaque problems are counted, and no weighting of transparent problems is equivalent to that restricted total. When importance attaches to the propositions learned, a weighting is extensional exactly when the rule it favours leaves credences unchanged on learning nothing new. The remaining dispute concerns only weights that depend on the state at which a proposition is learned.

## 1. Introduction

Accuracy arguments for conditionalization compare updating plans by their expected accuracy. Greaves and Wallace (2006) showed that when an agent's learning is transparent, so that whenever she learns $p$ she also learns that $p$ is what she learned, conditionalization has the highest expected accuracy among the plans that respond only to what is learned. Without transparency the result fails. Schoenfield (2017) showed that the best such plan in general conditions on the proposition that one has learned $p$. Externalists about evidence hold that learning is sometimes opaque, so they face a choice. They can give up the accuracy argument or give up conditionalization. Recent responses have explored both options (Das 2019; Isaacs and Russell 2023; Easwaran and Nielsen 2025).

Meehan and Zhang (2025) offer a third option. They impose a normative constraint, *global evidential constancy*: same prior over states, same evidence, same posterior, whatever learning situation one is in (314–315). They then evaluate rules by their *total* expected accuracy, summed over all factive updating problems that share a prior space (329). Conditionalization uniquely maximizes this total among globally evidentially constant rules (their Fact 10, 330). They then consider an objection they take seriously: why should every updating problem count equally? They answer with a characterization. Conditionalization is optimal for a weighting of updating problems if and only if the weighting is *extensional* (Fact 11, 337). They end the paper uncertain about this condition. They say they are "getting close to the bedrock of normative explanations" (337). They note "a strong connection between extensionality on the one hand, and evidence internalism on the other" (338). And they conclude that "the important question is not whether evidence internalism is true, but whether the (total) expected accuracy of an updating rule should be evaluated in a way that satisfies extensionality" (339).

This essay answers the two questions this ending leaves open: how extensionality is related to internalism, and whether it can be grounded in something more basic. My thesis is this:

> The extensionality on which Meehan and Zhang's vindication of conditionalization rests is neither disguised internalism nor bedrock. The vindication survives when only opaque updating problems are counted. And for weightings on which importance attaches to the propositions learned, extensionality is equivalent to the principle that learning nothing new should leave one's credences unchanged.

The argument has three parts. First, I show that Meehan and Zhang's own uniform total ranks globally evidentially constant rules exactly as a total over the transparent problems of learning whether $q$ does (Proposition 1). Their main theorem is therefore the Greaves–Wallace theorem summed over yes/no questions. That might suggest that the argument smuggles in internalism. It does not. The uniform total over opaque problems alone is also extensional, and no weighting of transparent problems is equivalent to it (Propositions 2 and 3). Second, I consider the weightings that Meehan and Zhang's objector has in mind, those that let importance vary with what is learned. For these weightings extensionality holds exactly when the favoured rule satisfies *No News*: on learning nothing new, keep the prior (Proposition 4). Third, I argue that this relocates the dispute. A non-Bayesian who wants a non-extensional weighting while respecting No News must let the weight of learning $p$ depend on which $p$-state one is in. That is the kind of information about how one learns that global evidential constancy excludes from the rule itself.

The increment over Meehan and Zhang is exact. They prove that extensionality is necessary and sufficient (Fact 11, with Lemmas 1 and 2, 344–345), and that a point mass on one problem is extensional iff the problem is transparent (Fact 12, 338). They note that weightings supported on transparent problems are extensional and that extensional weightings are closed under linear combination (336). They do not show the equivalence of the uniform total with the yes/no total, or that the opaque-only total is extensional and irreducible to transparent problems. Nor do they connect extensionality with No News. Proposition 3 shows that their claim that one need not be an internalist to accept extensionality (338) is true in a stronger sense than their examples show.

For readers outside formal epistemology the point is as follows. A defence of a rule "in general" must say which situations count and how much. Meehan and Zhang's choice of weights turns out to be equivalent to a simple internalist choice. But the defence also goes through on a choice that counts only the hard cases. And the obvious way to make the weights less arbitrary, by caring more about some things learned than others, preserves the defence exactly when it does not make the favoured rule change its mind on learning nothing.

## 2. The total-accuracy argument

I follow Meehan and Zhang's framework and notation. A *prior space* $(S,\pi)$ consists of a finite set of states $S$ with $|S| = n \ge 2$ and a regular probability function $\pi$ on $S$ (290). A *learning situation* $E$ maps each state $s$ to the strongest proposition learned at $s$, a subset of $S$ containing $s$. So evidence is factive, an assumption they adopt explicitly (290 n. 4). The triple $(S,\pi,E)$ is an *updating problem* (291). An *updating rule* takes an updating problem and returns, for each state, a posterior over $S$ (291). The problem is *transparent* if $E(s) \subseteq [E = E(s)]$ for every $s$, where $[E=E(s)]$ is the set of states at which one learns $E(s)$; otherwise it is *opaque* (292–294). For factive $E$, transparency coincides with partitionality (294 n. 5). Accuracy is measured by a strictly proper scoring rule $A$, where higher is better (296). The expected accuracy of a rule $f$ in a problem is $\sum_s \pi(s) A(f_{(S,\pi,E)}(s), s)$ (297).

Four rules matter here. BCOND recommends $\pi(\cdot \mid E(s))$. META-COND recommends $\pi(\cdot \mid E = E(s))$. The truth rule recommends certainty in the actual state. The dogmatic rule recommends $\pi$ (291–292). Meehan and Zhang argue that appeals to followability cannot explain why BCOND is rational under opacity. META-COND has the highest expected accuracy among evidentially constant rules in every problem (Fact 2, 300, from Schoenfield 2017). In *Dana*, an opaque problem about the weight of butter, the dogmatic rule has higher expected accuracy than BCOND under the Brier score (Fact 3, 302). And, given plausible principles about followability, mixtures of BCOND and META-COND do better than BCOND (303–306).

Their response has two parts. The first is global evidential constancy, the conjunction of evidential constancy and *noncontrastiveness*. Noncontrastiveness says that two problems with the same prior space that deliver the same proposition at a state get the same posterior there (309, 314–315). They defend it as a normative constraint, not a followability constraint (314, 317), chiefly through its equivalence with idempotence (Fact 6, 320). A rule is globally evidentially constant iff it is given by a map from a prior space and a proposition to a posterior (Fact 5, 316). I write $f(p)$ for its output on $p$, with the prior space fixed. BCOND and the dogmatic rule qualify; META-COND and the truth rule do not (316). Even so, every globally evidentially constant rule is beaten in some problem by another such rule (Fact 8, 325). So no rule is best problem by problem.

The second part is an evaluation of rules "in general". The *total expected accuracy* of $f$ relative to $(S,\pi)$ is its expected accuracy summed over the set $\mathcal{E}$ of all factive learning situations on $S$ (329). *Highest Total Expected Accuracy* (Hi-TEA) says that a rule is rational iff, among globally evidentially constant rules, it has the highest total expected accuracy relative to every prior space (330). Fact 10 says that BCOND does (330), and it does so uniquely (333). Meehan and Zhang read the total as an objective measure of a rule's accuracy for any agent, "irrespective of their uncertainties about their evidential situations" (330 n. 35).

In §5.3 they consider weighted totals. A *weighting function* $\lambda$ assigns each $E \in \mathcal{E}$ a non-negative weight, positive for some $E$, and the weighted total is
$$\mathrm{TEA}_\lambda(f) = \sum_{E\in\mathcal{E}} \lambda(E) \sum_{s} \pi(s)\, A\big(f_{(S,\pi,E)}(s), s\big)$$
(335). Each proposition $p$ induces the random variable $\lambda_p(s) = \sum_{E:\,E(s)=p} \lambda(E)$. The weighting is *extensional* if $\lambda_p(s)$ depends only on whether $p$ is true at $s$ (336). In their gloss, "how important it is to update accurately in response to learning p at s does not depend on anything other than the truth of p at s" (336). By factivity $\lambda_p(s) = 0$ when $s \notin p$, so extensionality says that $\lambda_p$ is constant on $p$. For globally evidentially constant $f$,
$$\mathrm{TEA}_\lambda(f) = \sum_s \pi(s) \sum_{p \ni s} \lambda_p(s)\, A(f(p), s) \qquad (\ast)$$
(345). Lemma 1 (344) says the optimal such rule recommends $\pi(\cdot\mid\lambda_p)$, where $\pi(s\mid\lambda_p) \propto \pi(s)\lambda_p(s)$. Lemma 2 (345) says this is $\pi(\cdot\mid p)$ for every $p$ iff $\lambda$ is extensional. Together they give Fact 11.

One further observation, which I use throughout. For every state $s$, $\sum_{p \ni s}\lambda_p(s) = \sum_{E}\lambda(E)$, because each learning situation delivers exactly one proposition at $s$. So $\lambda_p(s)$, rescaled by the total weight, is a probability distribution over the propositions true at $s$. I call the family $(\lambda_p(s))$ the *learning profile* of $\lambda$. By $(\ast)$, two weightings whose profiles are proportional rank all globally evidentially constant rules identically. I call such weightings *equivalent*. Lemma 1 then says that the optimal rule for $\lambda$ is conditionalization on the profile read as a likelihood. Extensionality says that, under this reading, which true proposition one learns at $s$ carries no information about $s$ beyond the truth of that proposition.

## 3. Not disguised internalism

### 3.1 The uniform total is a total over yes/no questions

For a proposition $q$ with $\emptyset \ne q \ne S$, the *yes/no problem* for $q$ is the learning situation $E_q$ with $E_q(s) = q$ if $s \in q$ and $E_q(s) = S\setminus q$ otherwise. The trivial problem $E_S$ delivers $S$ everywhere. Since $E_q = E_{S\setminus q}$, there are $2^{n-1}$ distinct yes/no problems, counting the trivial one. All are transparent. Let $\mathcal{Y}$ be this family.

**Proposition 1.** For every prior space $(S,\pi)$ with $|S|=n$ and every globally evidentially constant rule $f$,
$$\sum_{E\in\mathcal{E}} \sum_s \pi(s)\, A\big(f(E(s)),s\big) \;=\; 2^{(n-1)^2} \sum_{E \in \mathcal{Y}} \sum_s \pi(s)\, A\big(f(E(s)),s\big).$$

*Proof.* For the uniform weighting on $\mathcal{E}$, $\lambda_p(s)$ is the number of factive learning situations that deliver $p$ at $s$. Each of the other $n-1$ states may receive any of the $2^{n-1}$ propositions containing it, so $\lambda_p(s) = (2^{n-1})^{n-1}$ for every $s\in p$. This is Meehan and Zhang's constant $\alpha_S$ (344). For the uniform weighting on $\mathcal{Y}$, the only yes/no problem that delivers $p$ at a $p$-state is the one that asks whether $p$, or the trivial problem if $p=S$. So $\lambda_p(s) = 1$ for $s \in p$. The profiles are proportional, and $(\ast)$ gives the identity. ∎

**Corollary.** Fact 10 follows from Greaves and Wallace's theorem (Fact 1, 298). Each yes/no problem is transparent, so in each of them BCOND has the highest expected accuracy among evidentially constant rules, and every globally evidentially constant rule is evidentially constant. BCOND therefore maximizes every summand on the right of Proposition 1, and so it maximizes the left. Uniqueness follows because a rule that departs from $\pi(\cdot\mid p)$ does strictly worse, by strict propriety and regularity, in the problem that asks whether $p$.

Meehan and Zhang already use the problem of learning whether $p$ in their proof of Fact 7 (324–325), and they observe that the Greaves–Wallace result is a special case of Fact 11 (338). Proposition 1 gives the converse for their headline result. In a precise sense their uniform total is internalist: it cannot tell the externalist's world of opaque learning from a world in which one is asked random yes/no questions. The identity also gives a random-partition reading. Colour each state red or blue by a fair coin and learn the colour of the actual state. Then the block containing a given state is uniformly distributed over the $2^{n-1}$ propositions containing it, and the uniform total over all factive problems has exactly this profile. I checked Proposition 1 by enumerating all factive learning situations for $n = 3$ and $n=4$ (64 and 4,096 of them), and by exact rational arithmetic on random priors and rules. Using the same code I reproduced the totals in Meehan and Zhang's Tables 2 and 3 ($-2.72$, $-4.48$, $-6.8$; 331–332).

### 3.2 The vindication survives on opaque problems alone

Proposition 1 might seem to show that Hi-TEA does no work for the externalist that Greaves and Wallace had not already done. That would be too quick. The dialectically relevant test counts only the problems in dispute.

**Proposition 2.** Let $\lambda^{\circ}$ be the uniform weighting on the opaque problems in $\mathcal{E}$. Then $\lambda^\circ_p(s) = 2^{(n-1)^2} - B_{n-|p|}$ for every $s\in p$, where $B_m$ is the $m$-th Bell number. So $\lambda^\circ$ is extensional, and BCOND uniquely maximizes total expected accuracy over opaque problems alone.

*Proof.* A transparent problem that delivers $p$ at $s$ is a partition of $S$ with $p$ as a cell, and there are $B_{n-|p|}$ such partitions. Subtract this from the count in Proposition 1. The result is positive and depends only on $|p|$. Fact 11 gives optimality, and Lemma 1 gives uniqueness, since every $\lambda^\circ_p$ is positive on $p$. ∎

The same subtraction gives a general fact. For any weighting $\lambda$, its restriction to opaque problems is extensional iff $\lambda$ is, because the transparent part is extensional (336) and differences of extensional weightings are extensional.

**Proposition 3.** For $n \ge 3$, no weighting supported on transparent problems is equivalent to $\lambda^\circ$.

*Proof.* Let $\mu$ be supported on transparent problems, that is, on partitions. Any partition that has $S\setminus\{s\}$ as a cell also has $\{s\}$ as a cell. So $\mu_{\{s\}}(s) \ge \mu_{S\setminus\{s\}}(t)$ for $t \ne s$, and the inequality survives rescaling. But $\lambda^\circ_{\{s\}}(s) = 2^{(n-1)^2} - B_{n-1}$ is less than $\lambda^\circ_{S\setminus\{s\}}(t) = 2^{(n-1)^2} - 1$, since $B_{n-1} \ge 2$ when $n\ge 3$. ∎

A two-problem case shows the same thing concretely.

> *Cyclic Informants.* There are three states, 1, 2 and 3. Informant A says "1 or 2" in state 1, "2 or 3" in state 2 and "3 or 1" in state 3. Informant B says "1 or 3" in state 1, "1 or 2" in state 2 and "2 or 3" in state 3. Give each informant's problem weight 1 and every other problem weight 0.

Both problems are opaque, since each report is true at two states but given at only one. The weighting is extensional: each two-state report has weight 1 at both of its states. It is not equivalent to any transparent weighting, because $\lambda_{\{3\}}(3) = 0 < \lambda_{\{1,2\}}(1) = 1$. The extensional profiles, rescaled, form the polytope of non-negative vectors $(c_p)$ with $\sum_{p\ni s} c_p = 1$ for every $s$. The transparent weightings fill out only the convex hull of the partitions inside it. *Cyclic Informants* is a vertex outside that hull, and $\lambda^\circ$ is another point outside it.

So the connection between extensionality and internalism that Meehan and Zhang note is real but limited. Fact 12 and the closure of extensional weightings under mixtures make every transparent weighting extensional. Proposition 1 shows that their own weighting is equivalent to a transparent one. But Propositions 2 and 3 show that the vindication does not depend on that equivalence. An evaluator who counts only opaque problems still finds BCOND uniquely best in general, and no weighting of transparent problems reproduces that verdict. This strengthens Meehan and Zhang's position. Their remark that one "does not have to be an internalist to subscribe to extensionality" (338) holds in the stronger sense that the externalist's own problems, taken by themselves, deliver Bayes.

## 4. Not bedrock

### 4.1 Importance that attaches to what is learned

The objection Meehan and Zhang take most seriously says that problems differ in importance. "[P]erhaps the updating problem where one learns the truth of some laws of nature is more important than one where one learns the number of stones in Wisconsin" (335; compare Levinstein 2019 on varying importance). Their reply is Fact 11. Some importance weightings are extensional, and when they are not, the non-Bayesian may simply insist that how important it is to update accurately depends on more than truth (337–338).

To see what the objection's weightings look like, notice that the uniform weighting has a product form. Normalized, it is the distribution of a random learning situation in which a true proposition is chosen independently at each state, uniformly among the true propositions there. The natural way to let importance vary with what is learned keeps this form and changes only the weights. Let $h$ assign each non-empty proposition a positive importance and put
$$\lambda^{\times}_h(E) = \prod_{s\in S} h(E(s)), \qquad \lambda^{+}_h(E) = \sum_{s\in S} h(E(s)),$$
a multiplicative and an additive aggregation. The uniform weighting is the case $h \equiv 1$ of each. Let $H(s) = \sum_{p\ni s} h(p)$ be the total importance of the truths at $s$. I say a rule satisfies *No News* if, on learning $S$ (nothing new), it recommends the prior.

**Proposition 4.** Let $\lambda$ be $\lambda^\times_h$ or $\lambda^+_h$, taken over all factive problems or over opaque problems only. The following are equivalent:

(i) $\lambda$ is extensional;

(ii) $H$ is constant on $S$;

(iii) the rule with the highest $\lambda$-weighted total expected accuracy among globally evidentially constant rules satisfies No News.

Moreover, the optimal rule for $\lambda^\times_h$ over all factive problems conditionalizes the *tilted prior* $\pi_H(s) \propto \pi(s)/H(s)$ on what is learned.

*Proof.* The profiles are computed as in Proposition 1. For the product form, $\lambda^\times_p(s) = h(p)\prod_{t\ne s} H(t) = Z\, h(p)/H(s)$, where $Z=\prod_t H(t)$. For the additive form, with $N = 2^{(n-1)^2}$, $\lambda^+_p(s) = N\,[\,h(p) + 2^{-(n-1)}(\sum_t H(t) - H(s))\,]$. If (ii) holds, both are constant on $p$, which is (i). (i) implies (iii) by Fact 11, since BCOND satisfies No News. For (iii) implies (ii): by Lemma 1 and strict propriety the optimal rule's response to $S$ is $\pi(\cdot \mid \lambda_S)$. By regularity this equals $\pi$ iff $\lambda_S$ is constant, and in both forms $\lambda_S(s)$ is strictly decreasing in $H(s)$. For opaque problems only, subtract the transparent part. That part is extensional, and its weight on learning $S$ is the weight of the trivial problem, which is the same at every state. So both conditions transfer. The tilted form follows from Lemma 1, since $\pi(s)\lambda^\times_p(s) \propto \pi(s)/H(s)$ on $p$. ∎

I verified Proposition 4 by enumeration for $n = 3$ and $n=4$ for both forms and both domains. The checks used random importance functions and balanced ones, including balanced functions that are not symmetric under relabelling states.

> *Enough Butter.* Dana's prior over 15, 16 and 17 oz. is $(0.2, 0.6, 0.2)$ (300–301). Suppose it matters especially to learn that there is at least 16 oz., enough for the recipe: $h(\{16,17\}) = 5$, and $h = 1$ for every other proposition.

Then $H = (4, 8, 8)$ and the tilted prior is $(1/3, 1/2, 1/6)$. The importance-weighted standard favours a globally evidentially constant rule that responds to learning nothing new by moving from $(0.2,0.6,0.2)$ to $(1/3,1/2,1/6)$. On learning $\{15,16\}$ it recommends $(2/5,3/5,0)$ rather than BCOND's $(1/4,3/4,0)$. Consider Noah's problem (312), in which nothing is ever learned. Under the Brier score Noah expects this rule to score $-1.18$, against $-1.12$ for keeping his prior. Since the rule is globally evidentially constant, it must give Noah this posterior too.

The source of the shift is easy to see. The important proposition $\{16,17\}$ is true at 16 and 17 and not at 15. So in the weighted population of problems, learning nothing is relatively more common at 15, where nothing important is there to be learned. The optimal rule treats learning nothing as evidence for 15.

A corollary identifies the harmless kind of importance. If $h(p) = h(S\setminus p)$ for every proper $p$, so that importance attaches to *questions* rather than to particular answers, then $H$ is constant. Exactly one of $p$ and $S\setminus p$ contains any given state, so $H(s) = h(S) + \sum h(\{p, S\setminus p\})$ for every $s$. Meehan and Zhang's laws-versus-stones example is naturally read in this way, as being about the importance of the question whether some law holds. On that reading it leaves extensionality, and Bayes, in place. Imbalance comes from caring more about one answer to a question than about the other, as in *Enough Butter*.

### 4.2 From bedrock to No News

Proposition 4 replaces the stand-off Meehan and Zhang describe with a sharper choice. They imagine a Bayesian who insists that extensionality "cuts epistemology at its joints". Against this Bayesian stands a non-Bayesian for whom "it also matters how likely it is for one to learn p at s", and perhaps also how important $p$ is (337–338). For weightings of the kind just defined, the non-Bayesian's alternative is one on which the best rule changes the agent's credences when she learns nothing. No News is not a Bayesian principle. The dogmatic rule satisfies it, and so do the optimal rules for many weightings that are not extensional (§5). Meehan and Zhang appeal to it in the case of Noah and Dana, where BCOND "recommends that both Noah and Dana retain their prior" when they learn nothing new (312–313), and their defence of idempotence rests on a related thought (319). For anyone who already accepts global evidential constancy it has an accuracy rationale as well. A rule's response to $S$ is also its response in Noah's transparent problem, and there, by strict propriety, any posterior other than $\pi$ is expected by the agent to be less accurate.

Put more abstractly, product-form weightings with state-dependent factors, $\lambda(E) = \prod_s h_s(E(s))$, realize every learning profile: take $h_s(p)$ proportional to the profile at $s$. The substantive restriction in Proposition 4 is therefore that the factor not depend on the state, so that the relative weight of two propositions is the same at every state where both are true. Given that restriction, extensionality is No News. What is left of the non-Bayesian's position is a weighting on which the weight of learning $p$ depends on which $p$-state obtains, beyond anything that the importance of $p$ can explain. That is exactly the "how likely it is to learn $p$ at $s$" consideration: information about the shape of one's learning situation. Meehan and Zhang's slogan for noncontrastiveness is "same prior over S, same evidence about S, same posterior over S" (309). It bars the rule from responding to an agent's beliefs about how she learns. An evaluation that weights by state-dependent learning profiles reintroduces those beliefs into the standard that is used to choose the rule.

Lemma 1 makes this exact. Imagine a representative agent with prior $\pi$ over states. Her credence about which learning situation she faces is $\lambda$ rescaled to total 1, independent of the state, and she learns $p$ without learning which situation delivered it. If she applies META-COND to this enlarged problem, she conditions on the proposition that she learned $p$. Her posterior over states is then proportional to $\pi(s)\sum_{E:\,E(s)=p}\lambda(E) = \pi(s)\lambda_p(s)$. That is $\pi(\cdot\mid\lambda_p)$, the rule Lemma 1 selects. So for every weighting, the globally evidentially constant rule that Hi-TEA selects is the rule META-COND would recommend to an agent whose opinions about how she learns are given by the weighting. The rule is globally evidentially constant only because those opinions have been frozen into the standard. Extensional weightings are exactly the opinions about how one learns under which META-COND and BCOND agree for this agent. They are the hypotheses about learning that make no difference to the rational response. Meehan and Zhang note that aggregates of this form can be read as an agent's own estimate, and they reject that reading in favour of an objective one (330 n. 35). The observation gives their objective reading content. A standard meant to hold "irrespective of their uncertainties about their evidential situations" (330 n. 35) should not choose the rule that a particular such uncertainty would recommend. And the only weightings that avoid doing so are the extensional ones.

I do not claim that this argument is conclusive. A non-Bayesian can reply that every weighting, the uniform one included, is a hypothesis about how one learns: by Proposition 1 it is the hypothesis of random yes/no questions. The difference the Bayesian needs is between hypotheses that would change the rational response and hypotheses that would not. Whether the reasons for noncontrastiveness carry over to the choice of standard is the open question. But it gives the Bayesian a reason for extensionality that is more than a bare appeal to joints. This is where Meehan and Zhang's argument now needs its defence.

## 5. Objections and replies

**Objection 1: the product and additive forms are special.** Proposition 4 covers two aggregations. Importance could combine across states in many other ways.

*Reply.* Partly granted. The forms were not chosen for convenience. The product form is the one the uniform weighting already has, and with state-dependent factors it is fully general at the level of profiles, which by $(\ast)$ is the only level that matters. The restriction that does the work is state-independence of the factors, which is what it means for importance to attach to propositions. The additive form shows that the result does not depend on multiplication. Other aggregations are not covered. Since extensionality is a system of linear equations in the weights, a generic weighting violates it. What Proposition 4 adds is that, in the natural family, violating it has a specific and unattractive cost.

**Objection 2: No News begs the question against the externalist.** In *Dana*, META-COND makes Dana certain of 16 oz. when she learns nothing new. So the externalist who rejects BCOND will reject No News too.

*Reply.* META-COND is not globally evidentially constant (316). The non-Bayesian in Meehan and Zhang's stand-off has granted global evidential constancy and disputes only the weights (335). Among globally evidentially constant rules No News does not favour BCOND. The dogmatic rule satisfies it, and so does the optimal rule for any non-extensional weighting with $\lambda_S$ constant. For example, take three states $a$, $b$, $c$ and add to the uniform weighting extra weight on the opaque problem in which one learns $\{a\}$ at $a$, $\{a,b\}$ at $b$ and $\{c\}$ at $c$. That problem never delivers $S$, so the weight on learning $S$ is unchanged and No News holds. But $\lambda_{\{a,b\}}$ is now larger at $b$ than at $a$, so the weighting is not extensional. What No News excludes is the importance-based route to non-extensionality, and nothing more.

**Objection 3: some problems matter more because they are the ones agents face.** Dana's actual problem is more important to Dana than Noah's (334–335). A weighting that concentrates on it is not of product form, and it violates neither No News nor anything else I have said.

*Reply.* A point mass on Dana's problem does violate No News. Dana learns $S$ only at 16 oz., so $\lambda_S$ is not constant, and the optimal rule is one of the one-shot rules that Meehan and Zhang discuss (325–326, 337). But the general point stands. Weightings that favour the problems agents actually face are state-dependent in the sense of §4.2: they encode how particular agents learn. Meehan and Zhang raise Dana's actual problem within the same objection as the laws-versus-stones example (335). Their own example of a non-extensional weighting is a point mass on an opaque problem (336–337). Their answer to agent-relative weighting is that total expected accuracy measures a rule's accuracy for any agent (330 n. 35), together with the suggestion that a "low-level" theory of rationality may track such details (338). My results do not strengthen that answer. They show that once agent-relative facts about how one learns are set aside, the remaining reason to depart from extensionality, varying importance of what is learned, comes at the price of No News.

**Objection 4: why sum at all?** A cautious evaluator might rank rules by their worst expected accuracy across problems, and then no weights are needed.

*Reply.* This standard does not favour conditionalization. Since a factive learning situation chooses the proposition learned at each state independently, the worst case for a globally evidentially constant rule $f$ is $\sum_s \pi(s)\min_{p\ni s} A(f(p),s)$. This is at most $\sum_s \pi(s) A(f(S),s)$, which by propriety is at most $\sum_s \pi(s)A(\pi,s)$, the value of the dogmatic rule. So the dogmatic rule is worst-case optimal. With Dana's prior and the Brier score, BCOND's worst case is $-1.188$ and the dogmatic rule's is $-1.12$. BCOND's worst-case problem is Dana's own, as Meehan and Zhang's Table 3 shows (332). Any vindication of conditionalization along these lines must therefore aggregate by weights, and the questions of §§3–4 cannot be avoided.

## 6. Conclusion

Meehan and Zhang show that their argument for conditionalization stands or falls with extensionality. I have shown three things about that condition. Their uniform total is equivalent to a total over transparent yes/no questions, so Fact 10 is the Greaves–Wallace theorem summed. The argument still goes through when only opaque problems are counted, and that verdict has no internalist equivalent. And for weightings on which importance attaches to the propositions learned, extensionality is equivalent to No News. These results narrow the open question. Meehan and Zhang locate it in a choice between extensionality and a non-Bayesian's incredulous stare. The choice that remains is whether a standard for evaluating rules in general may weight learning $p$ differently at different $p$-states. I have argued that the motivation for noncontrastiveness bears on that question. I have not shown that it settles it. My results are for finite state spaces and factive evidence, the setting of Meehan and Zhang's paper. Extending them to infinite settings is left open (compare Easwaran and Nielsen 2025).
