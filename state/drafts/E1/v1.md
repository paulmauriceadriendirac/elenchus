# Ruling Out the Incomparable: Independent Sources and the Structure of Normality-Based Knowledge

**Abstract.** Normality theories of inductive knowledge hold that one can know that a possibility does not obtain if it is sufficiently less normal than one's actual situation. I argue that knowledge from independent sources requires more: one must sometimes rule out possibilities that are not less normal than one's actual situation but incomparable with it. Given an anti-skeptical premise and a principle insulating what one learns from one instrument against the undetected failure of another, independent instrument, every theory that confines exclusion to less normal possibilities is inconsistent. Such theories include the i-normality completion of Goodman and Salow's framework and every completion that assumes comparability, the probabilist one among them. The result is minimal and machine-checked. Its logical signature is the .3 axiom, which the case falsifies. Along the way I prove that k-normality knowledge has exactly the logic S4.2, as Goodman and Salow claimed, and S4.3 under comparability.

## 1. Introduction

A thermometer that works gives you knowledge of the temperature, even though it could have been off, and even though you cannot tell from its reading that it is not. Normality theories explain this. Goodman and Salow's version takes as its guiding idea that "if a possibility is sufficiently less normal than one's actual situation, then one can know that that possibility does not obtain" (Goodman and Salow 2023). Your thermometer is working well, so the possibility that it is three degrees off is much less normal than your actual situation, and you can rule it out. The framework has become the most developed account of how inductive knowledge outruns evidence, and it now comes in several versions. They differ in how normality is ordered: on a single scale (as when normality is the probability of answers to a contextually supplied question), or along several dimensions that need not be comparable. They also differ in how knowledge is computed from normality (the "completions" called i-, k- and km-normality). Goodman and Salow present the single-scale and the multi-dimensional pictures as rival developments and say that adjudicating between them is a question for further work, one that turns on "whether a reasonably constrained account of the needed context-sensitivity can be given" (2023, §5).

This paper asks what such theories say when knowledge comes from two independent sources at once. My thesis is this:

> Knowledge from independent sources requires that one sometimes rule out possibilities that are not less normal than one's actual situation, but incomparable with it. Hence any normality theory on which one can rule out only less normal possibilities makes knowledge from one source hostage to the undetected failure of another, independent source.

The theories so confined include i-normality, whatever the normality ordering, and every completion under comparability, including the probabilist one. The argument is a short impossibility result (§4). An anti-skeptical premise, a principle I call Insulation, and the confinement of exclusion to less normal possibilities cannot all hold. The premises are independent, and the set is minimal: drop any one and the rest are jointly satisfiable. I checked the result by computer. The incompatibility has a logical signature (§5). Confined theories validate the modal axiom .3, which says that the knowledge states one cannot rule out are linearly ordered, and the case refutes .3 directly. In proving this I also settle a claim left unproved in the literature. Goodman and Salow (2018, n. 10) state that the logic of knowledge in their KK-validating normality model is S4.2, the logic Stalnaker (2006) advocates. I prove that the k-normality logic of knowledge is exactly S4.2, and that it is exactly S4.3 once comparability is added.

The thesis does not say that normality theories fail. It says which of them fail for multi-source knowledge, and why: the fault lies in confining exclusion to less normal possibilities, a feature shared by the probabilist and the i-normality versions. What survives in a single context is multi-dimensional k-normality. I do not claim that it is correct, and §6.4 states its main cost. Nor does the thesis concern normality theories of justification, such as normic support (Smith 2016), since it turns on what one knows at worlds where one's beliefs are partly false.

Here is the increment over the closest prior work. Goodman and Salow (2023, §5) leave open the choice between comparable and multi-dimensional normality. Ahmadian (2026) shows that comparability is independent of the other axioms in her axiomatization of normality structures, so it can be dropped without disturbing them. Loets (2022) argues in general terms that strong normality logics rest on substantive assumptions with unwelcome consequences. What follows is a specific argument against comparability, drawn from a structural feature of knowledge rather than from verdicts about particular probabilities. It extends to i-normality whatever the ordering. It has an exact modal signature, and it comes with the first published proof, so far as I have found, of the S4.2 claim, together with its S4.3 strengthening. The argument also differs from the multi-question problem Fang (2026) raises for question-sensitive accounts. Mine arises within a single question, and it applies to theories that are not question-sensitive at all.

Why it matters beyond the debate's participants: whether all of one's inductive knowledge can be ranked on a single scale of "how well things went" is a basic question about how knowledge from different sources combines. If the answer is no, then any theory that grounds knowledge in a single measure of normality, probabilistic or not, misdescribes ordinary knowledge from more than one instrument, witness or sense. §2 sets out the framework, §3 gives the case and the premises, §4 proves the result, §5 gives its logical signature, and §6 answers objections.

## 2. The framework

I use only the parts of Goodman and Salow's framework the argument needs, and I state them in my own notation. Fix a body of evidence and let $E$ be the set of worlds compatible with it, assuming evidence is transparent, so that all worlds in $E$ share it. On $E$ there are two relations. $u \succeq v$ ("$u$ is at least as normal as $v$") is a preorder, and $u \succ v$ abbreviates "$u \succeq v$ and not $v \succeq u$". $u \gg v$ ("$u$ is sufficiently more normal than $v$") meets two conditions:

(S1) If $u \gg v$, then $u \succ v$.

(S2) If $z \gg u$ and $u \succeq v$, then $z \gg v$.

(S1) records that "sufficiently more normal" entails "more normal". (S2) is a chaining condition in the spirit of those the framework imposes (Goodman and Salow 2021): anything sufficiently more normal than $u$ is sufficiently more normal than whatever $u$ is at least as normal as. Only Proposition 2 below uses it; the main result does not. The believed worlds are those not sufficiently less normal than some evidential possibility:

$$R_B = \{v \in E : \text{there is no } u \in E \text{ with } u \gg v\}.$$

I assume $R_B$ is non-empty, which holds in every finite structure. Of the three completions Goodman and Salow (2023) consider, two can be stated directly. On **i-normality** one knows at $w$ whatever is true throughout

$$R_K^i(w) = \{v \in E : \text{not } w \gg v\},$$

so one rules out exactly the possibilities sufficiently less normal than one's actual world. On **k-normality** one knows whatever is true throughout

$$R_K^k(w) = R_B \cup \{v \in E : v \succeq w\},$$

that is, the believed worlds together with every evidential possibility at least as normal as actuality. This is the form of the KK-validating model of Goodman and Salow (2018). The third completion, km-normality, adds margins. Goodman and Salow show that under comparability it coincides with i-normality (2023). **Comparability** is the condition that for all $u, v \in E$, $u \succeq v$ or $v \succeq u$. It holds in the probabilist development, on which the normality of a world is the evidential probability of its answer to a contextually supplied question (Goodman and Salow 2021; 2023, §5), because probabilities are totally ordered.

The notion that does the work is this:

(SC) *Strong Confinement.* If $v \in E$ and $v \notin R_K(w)$, then $w \succ v$.

Strong Confinement says that one can rule out, among the possibilities one's evidence leaves open, only possibilities that are less normal than one's actual world. The guiding idea says that being *sufficiently* less normal suffices for exclusion. Strong Confinement says that being less normal is necessary.

**Lemma 1.** i-normality satisfies SC. k-normality satisfies SC given comparability. *Proof.* If $v \notin R_K^i(w)$ then $w \gg v$, so $w \succ v$ by (S1). If $v \notin R_K^k(w)$ then not $v \succeq w$. Under comparability $w \succeq v$, so $w \succ v$. ∎

k-normality without comparability does not satisfy SC: it excludes any non-believed world that is not at least as normal as actuality, and such a world may be incomparable with actuality.

**Lemma 2** (*No Mutual Exclusion*). Under SC, if $v \notin R_K(w)$ then $w \in R_K(v)$. *Proof.* If both exclusions held, SC would give $w \succ v$ and $v \succ w$, which is impossible. ∎

So in any strongly confined theory, two worlds sharing one's evidence are never each able to rule out the other.

## 3. Twin Thermometers

> *Twin Thermometers.* Two rooms, A and B, are thermally isolated from each other. Each contains a thermometer. The two instruments are of the same model but were made in different factories and calibrated separately, so their errors are independent. Both read 20°. Nothing in the agent's evidence bears on whether either is accurate beyond the readings themselves.

Classify each thermometer's actual error as N (within half a degree), M (between half a degree and two degrees) or F (more than two degrees: far off). The worlds compatible with the evidence fall into nine classes $\langle a, b \rangle$, where $a$ is the error class of A's thermometer and $b$ that of B's. Since the readings are fixed, a world's class settles the temperatures to the relevant grain. The probability that a given instrument is far off is small, say $\varepsilon = 0.01$, so the probability that neither is far off is about 0.98.

The first premise is a minimal anti-skepticism.

(AS) At $\langle N, N \rangle$, the agent knows that neither thermometer is far off.

Every normality theory is built to deliver (AS). When things go as they normally do, a working instrument yields knowledge that it is not wildly wrong. The conjunction is highly probable, so (AS) raises no preface or lottery worry.

The second premise is the crux.

(INS) *Insulation.* If at $\langle N, N \rangle$ the agent knows that B's thermometer is not far off, then she knows this at $\langle F, N \rangle$. Likewise with A and B exchanged: if at $\langle N, N \rangle$ she knows that A's thermometer is not far off, she knows it at $\langle N, F \rangle$.

Consider the agent at $\langle F, N \rangle$. Room A is in fact 23°, and her belief that it is about 20° is false. Room B is about 20°, as she believes. Compared with $\langle N, N \rangle$, nothing has changed in how her belief about B was formed, in the evidence on which it rests, or in how well the process producing that evidence performed. B's thermometer performed exactly as in the best case. The only difference is the performance of a different instrument, in a different room, whose errors are independent of B's, and of whose failure she has no evidence. Insulation says that this difference does not cost her the knowledge about B that she would otherwise have had.

Three considerations support Insulation. First, the verdict is the one we give without hesitation in ordinary multi-source cases. If you read the time from an accurate clock and weigh flour on a scale that happens to be miscalibrated, you know what time it is; the scale's failure is no reason to doubt it. Second, Insulation is weak. It concerns a single proposition, that B's thermometer is not far off, and a single pair of worlds. It does not say that what one knows about B is wholly independent of A, only that one piece of knowledge which the best case delivers survives an independent failure elsewhere. Third, Insulation is restricted to independent sources. Where the sources share a cause of failure (the same factory, the same power surge), A's being far off makes B's failure more likely, and a normality theory may rightly let it matter. Twin Thermometers stipulates independence, and the probabilist's own model of the case, a product of identical error distributions, encodes it.

The case differs from Goodman and Salow's repeated weighing of a single object (2023), in which two readings bear on one quantity and a sufficiently discrepant second reading can defeat knowledge from the first. Here the two readings bear on logically and causally independent quantities, and nothing in the evidence is discrepant.

## 4. The confinement theorem

**Theorem 1.** (AS), (INS) and (SC), applied to the knowledge relation of Twin Thermometers, are jointly inconsistent.

*Proof.* By (AS), at $\langle N, N \rangle$ the agent knows that B's thermometer is not far off. By (INS) she knows this at $\langle F, N \rangle$. Since $\langle N, F \rangle$ is a world at which B's thermometer is far off, $\langle N, F \rangle \notin R_K(\langle F, N \rangle)$. Exchanging A and B, $\langle F, N \rangle \notin R_K(\langle N, F \rangle)$. The two worlds share the agent's evidence, since both instruments read 20° at each, so this mutual exclusion contradicts Lemma 2. ∎

By Lemma 1, the theorem refutes, for Twin Thermometers, every i-normality theory and every comparable theory, which includes km-normality under comparability and the probabilist development under any of the completions. On the probabilist view the verdict is easy to see. If the contextual question is "what are the temperatures in A and B?", the normality of $\langle F, N \rangle$ is $P(F)\,P(N)$, which equals that of $\langle N, F \rangle$. Equally normal worlds cannot exclude each other on any completion.

**Minimality.** Each premise is needed. Without (AS), let $\gg$ be empty: every world is believed, nothing is ruled out, and (SC) and (INS) hold, the latter vacuously. Without (INS), let $\langle N, N \rangle \gg v$ for every other world $v$, with all other worlds equally normal. Under i-normality this satisfies (S1), (S2), (SC) and (AS). Without (SC), take the multi-dimensional structure on which $\langle a, b \rangle \succeq \langle a', b' \rangle$ iff $a$ is at least as good an error class as $a'$ and $b$ at least as good as $b'$ (with N better than M and M better than F), and $\langle a, b \rangle \gg \langle a', b' \rangle$ iff $\langle a, b \rangle \succeq \langle a', b' \rangle$ and one coordinate drops from N to F. Under k-normality, $R_B$ is the four worlds without an F. At $\langle F, N \rangle$ the agent knows that B's thermometer is not far off and knows nothing about A's. At $\langle N, F \rangle$ the reverse holds. More generally, what she knows about each thermometer depends only on that thermometer's error class, which is Insulation in its strongest form.

I checked these claims by computer. Using the z3 solver, I encoded arbitrary preorders $\succeq$ and relations $\gg$ on the nine worlds subject to (S1) and (S2), together with the two completions. (AS) with (INS) is unsatisfiable under i-normality and under comparable k-normality, and satisfiable under k-normality without comparability, including when normality is required to be symmetric between the thermometers. A direct enumeration confirmed (S1), (S2) and the stated knowledge sets for the multi-dimensional model.

**Contagion.** Add a premise that the probabilist satisfies automatically and that is natural for any theory treating identical, independent instruments alike:

(SYM) Exchanging the roles of the two thermometers preserves $\succeq$ and $\gg$.

Under (SC) and (SYM), neither of $\langle F, N \rangle$ and $\langle N, F \rangle$ is more normal than the other, so each is compatible with what the agent knows at the other. At $\langle F, N \rangle$, then, she does not know that B's thermometer is not far off, and at $\langle N, F \rangle$ she does not know that A's is not. Strong Confinement does not merely force a choice about which instrument's failure spoils the other. It makes each failure spoil the other. The same reasoning extends to any number of identical, independent instruments. At a world where instrument $i$ is far off and instrument $j$ is not, the world that swaps them is equally normal, so it cannot be excluded, and at that world $j$ is far off. Under (SC) and (SYM), therefore, at any world where even one instrument is far off, the agent knows of no instrument that it is not far off. I call this contagion.

## 5. The logical signature

Theorem 1 has an exact modal counterpart, and stating it settles the logic of normality-based knowledge for the completions at issue. Read $\Box$ as knowledge, so that $\Box p$ holds at $w$ iff $p$ holds throughout $R_K(w)$. Consider the axiom

$$(.3)\qquad \Box(\Box p \to q) \lor \Box(\Box q \to p).$$

In epistemic terms, (.3) says that for any $p$ and $q$, either one knows that if one knows $p$ then $q$, or one knows that if one knows $q$ then $p$. Its failure has the form: for all I know, I know $p$ while $q$ is false, and for all I know, I know $q$ while $p$ is false.

**Proposition 1.** Under SC, $R_K$ is total on $E$: for all $w, v \in E$, $v \in R_K(w)$ or $w \in R_K(v)$. Hence (.3) is valid. *Proof.* Totality is Lemma 2. Suppose (.3) fails at $w$. Then there are $v_1, v_2 \in R_K(w)$ with $\Box p$ and $\neg q$ at $v_1$, and $\Box q$ and $\neg p$ at $v_2$. By totality, $v_2 \in R_K(v_1)$, which gives $p$ at $v_2$, or $v_1 \in R_K(v_2)$, which gives $q$ at $v_1$. Either way we have a contradiction. ∎

**Proposition 2.** With transparent evidence, (S1), (S2) and non-empty $R_B$, the logic of knowledge under k-normality is exactly S4.2 (S4 plus $\Diamond\Box p \to \Box\Diamond p$).

*Proof sketch.* Soundness. Reflexivity holds since $w \succeq w$. For transitivity, suppose $v \in R_K(w)$ and $u \in R_K(v)$. If $u \in R_B$, then $u \in R_K(w)$. Otherwise $u \succeq v$. If $v \succeq w$, then $u \succeq w$. If instead $v \in R_B$, then $u \in R_B$, since any $z \gg u$ would by (S2) satisfy $z \gg v$. Convergence holds because $R_B \subseteq R_K(v)$ for every $v \in E$. So every evidence cell is a reflexive, transitive, convergent frame, and S4.2 is sound. Completeness. It is a standard fact that S4.2 is complete with respect to finite rooted reflexive-transitive frames with a unique final cluster $C$. Given such a frame $\langle X, R \rangle$, set $u \succeq v$ iff $v R u$, and $u \gg v$ iff $u \in C$ and $v \notin C$. This satisfies (S1) and (S2), and it gives $R_B = C$ and $R_K^k(x) = C \cup \{v : x R v\} = R(x)$. So every such frame is a k-normality frame. ∎

**Proposition 3.** Adding comparability, the logic is exactly S4.3. *Proof sketch.* Under comparability, $R_K^k$ satisfies SC (Lemma 1), so (.3) is valid by Proposition 1. For completeness, S4.3 is complete with respect to finite chains of clusters, and the construction above turns any such chain into a comparable k-normality structure. ∎

I verified the frame conditions behind Propositions 1–3 by exhaustive enumeration of all 2,501 labelled single-cell structures satisfying (S1) and (S2) on up to four worlds. I also verified the representation step for every rooted reflexive-transitive convergent frame on up to four worlds (87 frames, 75 of them connected). This is a check on the proofs, not a substitute for them.

Two consequences follow. First, Goodman and Salow's S4.2 claim is correct for k-normality as such, and it is not correct for k-normality under comparability, where the logic is strictly stronger. Second, Twin Thermometers is a counterexample to (.3) as a principle about knowledge, and not merely to particular models. Let $p$ be "B's thermometer is not far off" and $q$ be "A's thermometer is not far off", and let the agent be at $\langle F, F \rangle$. She cannot rule out $\langle F, N \rangle$ or $\langle N, F \rangle$: on any reasonable ordering both are at least as normal as her actual situation, and neither i- nor k-normality lets one rule out a possibility at least as normal as actuality. By (AS) and (INS), $\Box p$ and $\neg q$ hold at $\langle F, N \rangle$, and $\Box q$ and $\neg p$ hold at $\langle N, F \rangle$. In words: for all she knows, she knows that room B is about 20° while A's thermometer is far off, and for all she knows, she knows that room A is about 20° while B's thermometer is far off. That is a coherent description of an agent with two independent instruments, and (.3) declares it impossible. The comparability question thus has a logical face. A normality theorist who wants KK and multi-source knowledge in a single context should want S4.2 and not S4.3, and so should reject comparability.

## 6. Objections and replies

### 6.1 Holism

*Objection.* Normality is a feature of whole situations. At $\langle F, N \rangle$ the agent's situation is abnormal, because one of her instruments has failed, and abnormal situations support less knowledge. Insulation simply denies this.

*Reply.* The contagion result shows what holism costs. Under (SYM), which any holist who treats identical, independent instruments alike accepts, a single far-off instrument deprives the agent of knowledge that any instrument is not far off. Abnormality somewhere is the normal condition of agents who rely on many sources. With a hundred independent instruments, each far off with probability 0.01, the chance that at least one is far off is about 0.63. On the holist reading, the framework's anti-skeptical predictions would therefore hold mainly for agents who consult few sources, or who are lucky in all of them. The holist can avoid this only by restricting which features of the world count toward the normality relevant to a given piece of knowledge. That is to give up holism, either for multiple dimensions of normality (§6.4) or for relativity to a question (§6.2). Nothing in the guiding idea requires holism. It says that sufficiently less normal possibilities can be ruled out. It does not say that only less normal ones can.

### 6.2 Question-relativity

*Objection.* On the probabilist development, normality is relative to a contextually supplied question. When only B's temperature is at issue, worlds that differ only in A's error fall into the same answer, so $\langle F, N \rangle$ and $\langle N, N \rangle$ are equally normal and the agent at $\langle F, N \rangle$ knows what she knows at $\langle N, N \rangle$. Insulation holds in that context, and the mutual exclusion of Theorem 1 holds across two contexts, which Lemma 2 does not forbid.

*Reply.* I grant the point about single-question contexts. The problem moves to the joint question, "what are the temperatures in A and B?" Such a question is either an admissible context or it is not.

If it is admissible, comparability holds within it, and by Theorem 1 and the contagion result, the agent at $\langle F, N \rangle$ does not know in that context that B's thermometer is not far off. Merely widening the question to include room A, about which she has no evidence of anything amiss, destroys her knowledge about room B. This is not the loss of knowledge that the contextual treatment of the preface paradox accepts (Goodman and Salow 2023, §5), and the difference matters. In a preface case the conjunction at issue is improbable, and the agent lacks knowledge of it even when everything is normal. Here the conjunction "neither thermometer is far off" has probability about 0.98, on any view that grants (AS) the agent at $\langle N, N \rangle$ knows it in the joint context, and the loss occurs only at worlds where the other instrument has in fact failed. The loss is caused by the actual condition of an irrelevant instrument, not by the shape of the question.

If the joint question is not admissible, there is no context in which the agent knows the highly probable conjunction that neither thermometer is far off, even at $\langle N, N \rangle$, so (AS) fails. She can know each conjunct in its own context but never both together. Anyone who must act on both rooms at once, by setting one thermostat or by comparing the two temperatures, would then have to act without knowledge that the evidence plainly supports. A theory that bans joint questions about independent matters to avoid this would be an instance of the unconstrained context-sensitivity that Goodman and Salow's condition warns against.

Either way, question-relativity pays a cost that multi-dimensional k-normality does not. The latter delivers (AS), (INS) and every crossed verdict within a single context.

### 6.3 Is Insulation too strong?

*Objection.* At $\langle F, N \rangle$ the agent falsely believes that A is about 20°. She therefore believes a false conjunction, and her belief about B is part of a body of belief that is in error. Perhaps that is enough to undermine it.

*Reply.* Insulation does not require her to know the conjunction, which is false and so unknowable. It requires that she know one conjunct, formed by its own instrument, true, and as well grounded as in the best case. That a false belief in one conjunct does not defeat knowledge of the other is not special to normality theories. If it did, almost no one would know anything, since almost everyone holds some false belief formed alongside their true ones. The objector might instead say that the A-reading is misleading evidence, and that misleading evidence defeats. But the A-reading is not evidence about B. Given independence, it is equally probable whatever B's error is, and the case has no discrepancy, of the kind present in repeated measurement, to do any defeating.

A related objection concerns correlation. Suppose A's failure did raise the probability that B is far off, as it would with a shared factory defect. Then Insulation could fail, and rightly. That is why (INS) is restricted to independent sources. Theorem 1 needs a single case in which Insulation holds, and the probabilist's own model of Twin Thermometers, with independent, identically distributed errors, is such a case.

### 6.4 What survives, and at what price

*Objection.* The only completion left standing in a single context is multi-dimensional k-normality. That inherits all the controversy over k-normality: it validates KK given transparency (Goodman and Salow 2023), it permits cliff-edge knowledge, and its KK version faces Hawthorne's (2025) independent objections. Moreover, the model in §4 has an odd feature of its own. At $\langle F, N \rangle$ the agent rules out $\langle F, M \rangle$. She therefore knows that if A's thermometer is far off then B's is spot on, a conditional linking two independent instruments.

*Reply.* Both points are correct, and they mark the limits of my claim. The odd conditional knowledge comes from k-normality's defining feature: at any world, one rules out every non-believed possibility not at least as normal as actuality. With incomparability in place, this includes worlds that are as bad as actuality on A's dimension and worse on B's. I do not defend that. My conclusion is negative and conditional. A normality theorist who wants (AS) and (INS) in a single context must reject Strong Confinement. Among the completions available, that means rejecting i-normality outright and rejecting comparability for k- and km-normality. The question is then which incomparable completion is best. My results leave one option untested: km-normality without comparability, whose margins might permit crossed exclusion without k-normality's conditional knowledge. I have not shown that it does. What such a completion must achieve can now be stated exactly. It must let a world rule out some possibilities that are incomparable with it in normality, which no strongly confined theory allows, without ruling out, as k-normality does, every non-believed world that is merely worse than actuality on some dimension.

The result also bears on a question Dutant and Rosenkranz (2024) leave open. They show that safety construed through a symmetric counterpart relation cannot meet certain closure demands, and they ask whether a non-symmetric relation, such as comparative normality, might do better. Proposition 1 identifies a cost on the asymmetric side. If exclusion is fixed by an asymmetric "more normal than" relation, accessibility is total, and two worlds can never exclude each other. Symmetric relations allow mutual exclusion freely, and Goodman and Salow reject simple symmetric safety for inductive knowledge on the ground that it wrongly predicts symmetric accessibility (2023, §2). What Twin Thermometers requires lies between these: a relation that is neither symmetric nor total.

## 7. Conclusion

I have argued that inductive knowledge from independent sources forces normality theories to let agents rule out possibilities that are not less normal than their actual situation, but incomparable with it. The argument is an impossibility result. Minimal anti-skepticism, Insulation and Strong Confinement are jointly inconsistent in a two-instrument case, and each pair of them is consistent. Given symmetry between identical, independent instruments, confinement produces contagion: one far-off instrument deprives the agent of knowledge about all the others. The modal signature of confinement is the .3 axiom, which the case falsifies. The k-normality logic of knowledge is exactly S4.2, as Goodman and Salow (2018) claimed, and it is S4.3 under comparability.

The result settles less than the full debate between single-scale and multi-dimensional normality, but more than a preference between them. It shows that i-normality and every comparable theory, the probabilist one included, cannot represent knowledge from two independent instruments within a single context. Question-relativity rescues them only at the price of contagion or the loss of highly probable conjunctive knowledge. It does not show that multi-dimensional k-normality is correct; that completion carries costs of its own. The next question is whether a margins-based completion without comparability can permit crossed exclusion without them.
