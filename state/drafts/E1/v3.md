# Gettier Knowledge across Independent Sources: K-Normality without Comparability

**Abstract.** Goodman and Salow's normality framework computes knowledge from relations of comparative normality. They argue that independent sources, such as a scale and a thermometer, tell against COMPARABILITY, and they model such sources with multi-dimensional normality. They also answer the worry that disjunctive characterizations of knowledge breed Gettier cases. On their view what matters is CONVEXITY, which their K- and KM-normality both satisfy. I show that this answer fails for K-normality on their own multi-dimensional model. Under COMPARABILITY the two disjuncts of K-normality are always nested, so its disjunctive form does no work. On products of independent respects the disjunction does work. It then credits knowledge of a disjunction of a justified false belief about one source and an unknown truth about another. This is not cliff-edge knowledge. At the single-malfunction situations of their model, K-normality agrees with justified-true-belief normality. KM-normality, by contrast, becomes non-disjunctive there. A principle that bans such knowledge is equivalent to a combination principle. Added to K-normality's knowledge default, it yields a completion that still validates KK.

## 1. Introduction

Goodman and Salow (2023) offer a general framework for inductive knowledge. What a person knows is fixed by her evidence and by two relations among the situations her evidence leaves open: being at least as normal, and being sufficiently more normal. The framework's five principles constrain knowledge without determining it, and Goodman and Salow compare three completions: I-NORMALITY, from Goldstein and Hawthorne (2022); K-NORMALITY, from Goodman and Salow (2018); and KM-NORMALITY, introduced in the 2023 paper. K-NORMALITY lets people know as much as the framework allows. Given transparent evidence it validates the KK principle, and it is the completion that defenders of KK have appealed to (Goodman and Salow 2023, 107 and n. 16).

Two parts of their discussion matter here.

The first is what they call "a powerful argument against COMPARABILITY" (119). COMPARABILITY is the principle that any two evidential possibilities are comparable in normality. Suppose a scale and a thermometer can fail independently. Then a situation in which only the scale fails, and one in which only the thermometer fails, must each be able to rule out the other, and COMPARABILITY forbids this. Their alternative makes normality multi-dimensional.

The second is their defence of the disjunctive form of K- and KM-NORMALITY against a diagnosis of Gettier cases that they attribute to Williamson (2015). On that diagnosis, "the blame rests on [the] disjunctive characterization of epistemic accessibility" (113). They argue that the better diagnosis is a failure of CONVEXITY, which K and KM both satisfy (114).

This paper puts the two together. My thesis:

> On Goodman and Salow's multi-dimensional account, K-NORMALITY credits knowledge of Gettier disjunctions: a justified false belief about one source disjoined with an unknown truth about another. It does so although it satisfies CONVEXITY, and although the knowledge is not cliff-edge knowledge. So their CONVEXITY reply protects KM-NORMALITY once COMPARABILITY fails, but it does not protect K-NORMALITY.

The argument runs as follows. Under COMPARABILITY, the two disjuncts of K-NORMALITY are always nested, so its disjunction does no work (Lemma 1). On multi-dimensional structures, KM-NORMALITY is non-disjunctive: one rules out a possibility exactly when changing actuality to it in one respect would yield a situation sufficiently less normal than actuality (Theorem 2). On the same structures, K-NORMALITY's knowledge about any single respect depends only on that respect, so K meets the anti-skeptical demand that motivates the multi-dimensional account. But K's disjunction becomes active exactly when one source has failed beyond what the agent believes and another is working better than the worst state she believes possible. The agent then knows a disjunction of the Gettier form without knowing either disjunct (Proposition 3). In Goodman and Salow's own model, K-NORMALITY coincides with justified-true-belief normality at the single-malfunction situations.

Two consequences follow. A principle that bans such knowledge, which I call SEPARATION, is equivalent to a principle of combination across respects (Proposition 4). This gives combination a motivation that Goodman and Salow's anti-skeptical argument does not supply. Adding SEPARATION to K-NORMALITY's knowledge default yields *meet-normality* (Proposition 5). Meet-normality validates KK, agrees with K-NORMALITY under COMPARABILITY, and agrees with it on every single-respect question.

Some things are not claimed here. K-NORMALITY is not refuted: a K-theorist can accept the knowledge as a cost. SEPARATION is not beyond dispute; the probabilist's joint contexts violate it. I do not decide MARGINS, KK or COMPARABILITY, and I say nothing about incomparability that is not multi-dimensional, such as over- and underestimation in Goodman and Salow's figure 3. The argument against COMPARABILITY is theirs.

Here is the increment over the closest work. Goodman and Salow's figure 4 depicts the verdict of K-NORMALITY that I examine, but without comment (119). Carter and Goldstein (2021), as Goodman and Salow report them (2023, 123 n. 30), characterize accessibility as not being sufficiently less normal along any dimension; Theorem 2 shows that KM-NORMALITY, defined from the two global relations alone, already says this on products. So far as I can find, four things are new: the nesting observation and its failure on products; the Gettier result and its bearing on Goodman and Salow's reply to Williamson; the equivalence of SEPARATION and combination; and meet-normality. Corollary 6 sharpens the context-by-context reply Goodman and Salow give at 121–123. The bearing on their suggested link between COMPARABILITY and MARGINS (§8) is modest, because they offer that link with explicit hedges (111).

Why should readers outside this debate care? Multi-dimensional normality protects knowledge from independent sources against skepticism. Combined with a knowledge-maximizing rule, that same move begins to generate the most familiar kind of non-knowledge. A theory of knowledge from several sources should not let a false belief about one source produce knowledge about how it relates to another.

## 2. The framework

I follow Goodman and Salow's formulations and page references. $R_E(w)$ is the set of situations evidentially accessible from $w$, and $R_K(w)$ the set epistemically accessible from it. One knows exactly what is true throughout $R_K(w)$ (2023, 91–92). The relations are $u \succeq v$ ("$u$ is at least as normal as $v$") and $u \gg v$ ("$u$ is sufficiently more normal than $v$"). They satisfy three conditions (92):
(i) $\succeq$ is reflexive and transitive;
(ii) $\gg$ is irreflexive and implies $\succeq$;
(iii) if $w_1 \succeq w_2 \gg w_3 \succeq w_4$, then $w_1 \gg w_4$.

It follows that $\gg$ is asymmetric and transitive. It also follows that $u \gg v$ excludes $v \succeq u$. Well-foundedness of $\gg$ is not assumed (92 n. 4).

The framework's five principles are these (92–93). FACTIVITY: $w \in R_K(w)$. EVIDENTIAL KNOWLEDGE: $R_K(w) \subseteq R_E(w)$. INDUCTIVE KNOWLEDGE: if $w \gg v$, then $v \notin R_K(w)$. INDUCTIVE LIMITS: an evidential possibility that no evidential possibility is sufficiently more normal than is epistemically accessible. CONVEXITY: an evidential possibility at least as normal as some epistemic possibility is epistemically accessible.

I assume TRANSPARENCY (100), so I work within one evidential cell $E$. Write $R_B = \{v \in E : \text{no } u \in E \text{ has } u \gg v\}$ for the doxastically accessible situations given by BELIEF. The framework runs belief and justified belief together (112–113). Write ${\uparrow}w = \{v \in E : v \succeq w\}$. By (iii), both $R_B$ and ${\uparrow}w$ are closed upward under $\succeq$.

The completions are these (104–105, 108–110, 113):

$$\textsc{I}:\ R_K(w) = \{v \in E : w \not\gg v\}$$
$$\textsc{K}:\ R_K(w) = R_B \cup {\uparrow}w$$
$$\textsc{KM}:\ R_K(w) = R_B \cup \{v \in E : \exists u \in E\,(v \succeq u,\ w \succeq u,\ w \not\gg u)\}$$
$$\textsc{JTB}:\ R_K(w) = R_B \cup \{w\}$$

Goodman and Salow characterize these completions by defaults (110). I-NORMALITY is the most inclusive relation compatible with EVIDENTIAL KNOWLEDGE and INDUCTIVE KNOWLEDGE. K-NORMALITY is the most restrictive relation compatible with FACTIVITY, INDUCTIVE LIMITS and CONVEXITY, a "knowledge default"; they introduce it as "the minimal change required to respect INDUCTIVE LIMITS" (104). KM-NORMALITY is the most restrictive relation compatible with MARGINS, INDUCTIVE LIMITS and CONVEXITY. JTB-NORMALITY, which identifies knowledge with justified true belief, is the most restrictive relation compatible with FACTIVITY and INDUCTIVE LIMITS; it fails CONVEXITY and is "vulnerable to Gettier-style counterexamples" (113).

MARGINS requires that an evidential possibility that is less normal than actuality, but not sufficiently less normal, be epistemically accessible (106). I-NORMALITY entails MARGINS (106, 111); so does KM-NORMALITY (108–110). K-NORMALITY entails KK given TRANSPARENCY (107). It also permits *cliff-edge knowledge*: knowing that one's situation lies on one side of the actual value of a quantity (105).

MARGINS therefore bears on KK in only one direction. Given failures of COLLAPSE it rules out the cliff-edge knowledge that KK requires in inexact cases. Rejecting MARGINS does not secure KK, and Goodman and Salow leave KK's status open (107 and n. 17).

COMPARABILITY says that any two evidentially accessible situations are comparable (101). Given COMPARABILITY, KM- and I-NORMALITY coincide (111).

## 3. Orthogonal errors

Goodman and Salow's argument runs as follows (2023, 117–118). Bjorn weighs himself and takes his temperature. "Plausibly, malfunctions in the two devices are epistemically independent" (117). If the thermometer is accurate, he knows his temperature even if the scale grossly misreports his weight, and the same holds with the instruments exchanged. The motivation is anti-skeptical: "allowing errors of one kind to generate ignorance about independent subject matters threatens much of the knowledge we take ourselves to have" (117).

Now take two situations with the same readings. In one only the scale is grossly wrong; in the other only the thermometer is. Each must be epistemically inaccessible from the other, yet they are mutually evidentially accessible. Given FACTIVITY and CONVEXITY, COMPARABILITY forbids this (107, 118).

Their alternative is multi-dimensional. For one situation to be at least as normal as another is for it to be at least as normal in every dimension. For it to be sufficiently more normal is for it to be also sufficiently more normal in some dimension (118).

Their model has two dimensions, each with three states: ordinary (0), odd (1) and bizarre (2). Ordinary is sufficiently more normal than bizarre. Odd is neither sufficiently less normal than ordinary nor sufficiently more normal than bizarre. I label the first coordinate the scale and the second the thermometer. Both K- and KM-NORMALITY make $(0,2)$ and $(2,0)$ mutually inaccessible. In this way, they say, the framework "can accommodate the kind of epistemic independence that anti-skeptical considerations arguably motivate" (118). I-NORMALITY fails here (119), which is why they reject it without COMPARABILITY (111).

The probabilist, developing Goodman and Salow (2021), replies by relativizing normality to a contextually supplied question (2023, 120–121). Goodman and Salow leave the choice between the two approaches open. In their words, it turns on "whether a reasonably constrained account of the needed context-sensitivity can be given" (117).

They also suggest that the open questions are linked (111). This "lends some abductive support to the biconditional" between COMPARABILITY and MARGINS. If COMPARABILITY fails, then "the question of whether MARGINS is true becomes the question of which of K-NORMALITY and KM-NORMALITY is true", and "K-NORMALITY is arguably preferable on grounds of its comparative simplicity". They add at once that "these are only some of the many considerations", and that all consistent combinations other than the ones they reject deserve "serious consideration".

## 4. K- and KM-NORMALITY on products

Call a structure *multi-dimensional* when $E = X_1 \times \dots \times X_n$, each respect $X_d$ carries a total preorder $\succeq_d$ and a relation $\gg_d$ satisfying (i)–(iii), and the global relations are defined from these as above; they then satisfy (i)–(iii). $w_d$ is the state of $w$ in respect $d$. For a set $S$ of respects, $w_S v$ is the situation that agrees with $w$ on $S$ and with $v$ elsewhere. Let $B_d = \{x \in X_d : \text{no } y \in X_d \text{ has } y \gg_d x\}$ and, for $x \in X_d$, ${\uparrow}x = \{y \in X_d : y \succeq_d x\}$; then $R_B = \prod_d B_d$ and ${\uparrow}w = \prod_d {\uparrow}w_d$. A proposition *concerns* a set of respects if its truth depends only on the states of those respects. Each $B_d$ and ${\uparrow}w_d$ is upward closed in a total preorder, and any two such sets are nested.

**Lemma 1.** Given COMPARABILITY, K-NORMALITY gives $R_K(w) = R_B$ if $w \in R_B$, and $R_K(w) = {\uparrow}w$ otherwise.

*Proof.* $R_B$ and ${\uparrow}w$ are upward closed in a total preorder, so they are nested. If $w \in R_B$, then ${\uparrow}w \subseteq R_B$. If $w \notin R_B$, then ${\uparrow}w \not\subseteq R_B$, so $R_B \subseteq {\uparrow}w$. ∎

So wherever COMPARABILITY holds, what a K-theorist's agent knows is either exactly what she believes or exactly that things are at least as normal as they actually are. The latter is her cliff-edge knowledge. The disjunction in the definition never yields knowledge of a disjunction of the two. Goodman and Salow's case for cliff-edge knowledge in the K style is a case for knowing ${\uparrow}w$: K-NORMALITY "provides a principled basis" for it (106).

**Theorem 2.** On every multi-dimensional structure, KM-NORMALITY gives
$$R_K(w) = \prod_d \{x \in X_d : w_d \not\gg_d x\} = \{v \in E : \text{for no } d \text{ does } w \gg w_{\{d\}^c} v\},$$
where $w_{\{d\}^c} v$ is $w$ with its state in respect $d$ changed to $v_d$.

*Proof.* Suppose first that $w_d \not\gg_d v_d$ for every $d$. Let $u$ take in each respect the less normal of $w_d$ and $v_d$. Then $w \succeq u$ and $v \succeq u$. Also $w \not\gg u$, since in each respect $u_d$ is either $w_d$ or $v_d$. So $v$ satisfies KM's second disjunct.

Conversely, suppose $w_d \gg_d v_d$. Then $v \notin R_B$. Moreover, whenever $v \succeq u$ and $w \succeq u$, we have $w_d \gg_d v_d \succeq_d u_d$, so $w \gg u$ by (iii). The second equality holds because $w \gg w_{\{d\}^c} v$ iff $w_d \gg_d v_d$. ∎

No finiteness is used. The form on the right uses only $\gg$ and is not disjunctive: it is I-NORMALITY applied to one-respect deviations from actuality.

**Proposition 3.** Let the structure be multi-dimensional with $R_B \neq \emptyset$, and let K-NORMALITY hold.

(a) At $w$, the states of respect $d$ left open are exactly $B_d \cup {\uparrow}w_d$. So knowledge about a single respect depends only on that respect's actual state.

(b) Say that COND holds at $w$ if there are distinct respects $d$ and $e$ such that $w_d \notin B_d$ and $w_e$ is strictly more normal than some $b \in B_e$. If COND fails, $R_K(w) = \prod_d (B_d \cup {\uparrow}w_d)$. If COND holds, then $R_K(w)$ is neither $R_B$, nor ${\uparrow}w$, nor any product of sets of states, and the agent knows

> (G) either respect $d$ is in a state she believes possible, or respect $e$ is at least as normal as it actually is,

while knowing neither disjunct. The first disjunct is false and believed; the second is true.

*Proof.* (a) $R_B$ and ${\uparrow}w$ are non-empty products, and the projection of a union is the union of the projections.

(b) Suppose COND fails. If $w \in R_B$, then $R_K(w) = R_B$, and ${\uparrow}w_d \subseteq B_d$ for each $d$. If instead $w_d \notin B_d$ for some $d$, then $B_d \subseteq {\uparrow}w_d$ by nesting. Every other respect $e$ has $B_e \subseteq {\uparrow}w_e$, because COND fails and $\succeq_e$ is total. So $R_B \subseteq {\uparrow}w$, and $R_K(w) = {\uparrow}w$. In both cases $R_K(w)$ is the stated product.

Now suppose COND holds. Then $R_B$ is contained in the first disjunct of (G) and ${\uparrow}w$ in the second, so (G) is known. The first disjunct is false since $w_d \notin B_d$, and believed since it contains $R_B$. The second is true since $w \in {\uparrow}w$. Choose $v \in R_B$ with $v_e = b$. Then $v \in R_K(w)$, and the second disjunct is false at $v$, so it is not known; FACTIVITY ensures the first is not known either. Now consider $w$ with its respect-$e$ state changed to $b$. It is not believed, because of respect $d$, and not in ${\uparrow}w$, because of respect $e$. So it lies outside $R_K(w)$, although each of its states appears in $R_K(w)$. Hence $R_K(w)$ is not a product. ∎

If $R_B = \emptyset$, as can happen when $\gg$ is not well-founded, K-NORMALITY gives ${\uparrow}w$, which is a product.

Part (a) matters for the dialectic. K-NORMALITY delivers in full the anti-skeptical independence that Goodman and Salow's argument demands: a failure in one respect never removes knowledge about another. Whatever is wrong with K on products, the anti-skeptical argument cannot show it. The difference lies entirely in knowledge that links respects.

## 5. A Gettier case that CONVEXITY does not exclude

Return to Goodman and Salow's model at $(0,2)$, where the scale is ordinary and the thermometer bizarre. $R_B$ is the four situations with no bizarre respect, and ${\uparrow}(0,2) = \{(0,0),(0,1),(0,2)\}$. So K-NORMALITY gives

$$R_K(0,2) = R_B \cup \{(0,2)\},$$

which is exactly what JTB-NORMALITY gives there. The same holds at $(2,0)$. Their figure 4 records the exclusion of $(1,2)$: it draws a double arrow from $(0,2)$ to $(1,2)$, which in their notation marks accessibility under KM- and I-NORMALITY but not under K-NORMALITY (105, 119). Proposition 3 says what the agent thereby knows:

> *Thermometer.* Bjorn's scale reads 175.4 pounds and is accurate. His thermometer reads 98.6 °F but is grossly wrong. He justifiably believes that neither instrument is grossly wrong. He cannot rule out a moderate error in the scale. According to K-NORMALITY he knows: *either the thermometer is not grossly wrong, or the scale has no moderate error.*

He believes the disjunction because he believes its first disjunct, which is false. It is true because of its second disjunct, which he does not know. The two disjuncts concern different instruments. This is the shape of Gettier's (1963) second case, where a justified false belief that Jones owns a Ford is disjoined with the claim, true by luck, that Brown is in Barcelona.

Goodman and Salow endorse the verdict that such disjunctions are not known. Against the justified-safe-belief account, they object that it predicts "any disjunction of a justified false belief and an unknown but safely true proposition will be known". They continue: "That prediction is false: such disjunctions are typically not known, despite being true and justifiably believed, for the same reason as in classic Gettier cases" (113–114). Their own Bjorn example is a justified true belief "that he weighs either around 175.4 pounds or exactly *x* pounds", which "is not something that he knows" (114). In *Thermometer*, K-NORMALITY counts as knowledge the analogous disjunction "nothing is bizarre, or things are exactly as they are". This is the disjunction JTB-NORMALITY counts as knowledge.

Their explanation of the Bjorn verdict does not carry over. In the one-dimensional case, CONVEXITY admits intermediate weights more normal than *x*, so the agent cannot know the disjunction (114). At $(0,2)$ nothing outside $R_B \cup \{(0,2)\}$ is at least as normal as a member of it, so JTB-NORMALITY satisfies CONVEXITY there. The situation whose inclusion would defeat the disjunction is $(1,2)$, and it is less normal than actuality. CONVEXITY never requires it. Goodman and Salow say that Williamson's disjunctive diagnosis, if correct, "would be trouble for K- and KM-NORMALITY, since they also characterize epistemic accessibility disjunctively" (113). They hold the trouble is avoided because CONVEXITY is the better diagnosis (114). On the multi-dimensional branch the trouble returns for K-NORMALITY, though CONVEXITY holds.

**The one-dimensional reply.** A defender of K will say that its cliff-edge knowledge always has this shape. Take an agent whose scale errs by $e$, beyond the belief bound $c$. K-NORMALITY lets her know that the error is at most $e$. That proposition is entailed by her false belief that the error is below $c$. It is true because the error lies between $c$ and $e$, which she does not know.

But that trivial decomposition is available for every piece of inductive knowledge in any situation outside belief, on every completion. Write any known $p$ as $R_B \vee (p \wedge \neg R_B)$. What distinguishes the multi-dimensional case is two things.

First, by Lemma 1 the one-dimensional agent knows ${\uparrow}w$ itself, that things are at least as normal as they actually are. That is the knowledge the K-theorist undertakes to defend. In *Thermometer* the agent knows no such thing. She does not know ${\uparrow}w$, because $(1,1)$ is open. By Proposition 3(a) she has no cliff-edge knowledge about either instrument. Her extra knowledge is a disjunction whose disjuncts are her false belief and the cliff-edge proposition she does not know. So the K-theorist's defence of cliff-edge knowledge does not cover it. The exclusion of $(1,2)$ violates MARGINS without giving the agent any cliff-edge knowledge.

Second, the disjuncts in (G) concern disjoint sets of respects. So (G) is not equivalent, given what she knows, to any proposition about either instrument. That is what makes the case Gettier's own pattern rather than a relabelling.

Two features of the case are not new, and I do not rely on them. K-NORMALITY loses this knowledge if the agent discovers its antecedent. That is an instance of the failures of NO DEFEAT that Goodman and Salow already document for K (127–128, 131). And they note that K can yield knowledge from a broken clock, given a special assumption about normality, which they count "an important consideration in favor of MARGINS" (114 n. 23). *Thermometer* needs no special assumption. It arises from the multi-dimensional structure itself, at the situations the orthogonality argument is about.

The argument of this section, regimented:

(P1) A completion that counts as known a disjunction of a justified false belief about one subject matter and an unknown truth about an independent subject matter, where the knowledge is neither cliff-edge knowledge nor licensed by INDUCTIVE KNOWLEDGE, counts a Gettier case as knowledge.
(P2) On Goodman and Salow's multi-dimensional model, K-NORMALITY counts such a disjunction as known whenever COND holds, as in *Thermometer*.
(P3) K-NORMALITY satisfies CONVEXITY.
(C) So satisfying CONVEXITY does not protect a disjunctive completion from Gettier verdicts, and Goodman and Salow's reply to the disjunctive diagnosis does not cover K-NORMALITY beyond COMPARABILITY.

(P1) restates the verdict Goodman and Salow endorse at 113–114, restricted to the non-trivial case. (P2) is Proposition 3 together with the two points just made: $(0,2) \not\gg (1,2)$, and the agent has no cliff-edge knowledge. (P3) is theirs (110).

KM-NORMALITY is immune. By Theorem 2 its accessibility sets are products. In any product, knowing a disjunction of propositions about disjoint sets of respects requires knowing one of them. The cases thus fit a refinement of the disjunctive diagnosis. What breeds the trouble is not that a characterization is disjunctive. It is that its disjuncts fail to nest. K's disjuncts nest under COMPARABILITY (Lemma 1), and KM is non-disjunctive on products (Theorem 2). Whether the refinement holds beyond these structures I leave open.

## 6. SEPARATION and combination

The verdict in *Thermometer* generalizes to a principle about respects that are independent in the normality structure:

> SEPARATION. If one knows $A \vee B$, where $A$ concerns only a set $S$ of respects and $B$ only the remaining respects, and $A$ is false but believed, then one knows $B$.

This principle is Goodman and Salow's claim about disjunctions of justified false beliefs and unknown truths, with two changes. It is restricted to disjuncts about independent subject matters, which is where the trivial decomposition cannot reach. And within that restriction, "typically" is strengthened to "always". It is vacuous for a single respect, so it does not touch one-dimensional cliff-edges. Nor does it forbid all knowledge that links respects. At $(0,0)$ it allows knowing that the two instruments are not both odd, since there no disjunct is false.

> COMBINATION. If $v \in R_K(w)$, and $S$ is a set of respects that includes one in which $w$ lies outside belief, then $w_S v \in R_K(w)$.

In words: if, for all one knows, the other sources are in a certain state, then for all one knows they are in that state while the sources that have failed beyond belief are as they actually are.

**Proposition 4.** On a multi-dimensional structure with $R_B \neq \emptyset$, a relation satisfying FACTIVITY satisfies SEPARATION at $w$ iff it satisfies COMBINATION at $w$.

*Proof.* Suppose COMBINATION holds, and that one knows $A \vee B$ as in SEPARATION. Since $A$ is believed, it contains $\prod_{s \in S} B_s$ (here $R_B \ne \emptyset$ is used); since it is false, some $s \in S$ has $w_s \notin B_s$. If some $v \in R_K(w)$ falsified $B$, then $w_S v \in R_K(w)$ would falsify both disjuncts.

Conversely, suppose $v \in R_K(w)$ and $w_S v \notin R_K(w)$, with $w_s \notin B_s$ for some $s \in S$. Let $A$ say that the respects in $S$ are not exactly as at $w$. $A$ is false, and it is believed, since $R_B$ excludes $w$'s state in respect $s$. Let $B$ say that the remaining respects are not exactly as at $v$. Any $x \in R_K(w)$ that falsifies $A$ satisfies $B$, or else $x = w_S v$. So $A \vee B$ is known, but $v$ shows that $B$ is not. ∎

Goodman and Salow's argument supports only the claim that a failure in one respect does not remove knowledge about another, and K satisfies that claim (Proposition 3(a)). So COMBINATION, which forbids failures from *adding* knowledge, does not follow from their argument. Proposition 4 gives it a different source: Goodman and Salow's own treatment of Gettier disjunctions, applied to independent subject matters. On multi-dimensional structures, KM-NORMALITY satisfies SEPARATION, by Theorem 2. K-NORMALITY violates it exactly when COND holds (Propositions 3 and 4). I-NORMALITY violates it at $(0,2)$, where it excludes $(2,2)$ and leaves $(2,0)$ open.

The scope restriction matters, and Goodman and Salow's own work marks it. They hold that knowledge from two scales weighing one person is not the conjunction of what each would give. A multi-dimensional treatment of the two scales "wrongly predicts" that it is, so even multi-dimensionalists about the scale and thermometer should reject such a treatment (129 n. 37; cf. Goldstein and Hawthorne 2022). SEPARATION is meant for respects that are independent subject matters, like weight and temperature. For those, by their lights, the product model is the right one. It is not meant for sources that bear on one quantity.

The probabilist rejects SEPARATION in joint contexts. I check this with the parameters of their figure 5 (122): in each respect each state is ten times as probable as the next worse one, the respects are independent, and the threshold is .95. Then the question about both instruments has $R_B = \{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0)\}$. At $(0,2)$, with I- or KM-NORMALITY, it excludes only $(2,2)$. So the agent knows "the thermometer is not bizarre or the scale is not bizarre" without knowing the second disjunct. With K-NORMALITY, the joint context also excludes $(1,2)$ at $(0,2)$. So it is not true that no probabilist context agrees with K's verdict; what is true is that, with a completion that respects MARGINS, neither the single-respect contexts nor the joint context excludes $(1,2)$ there.

Yet the K-probabilist's verdict differs in kind from *Thermometer*. The joint ordering is total, so by Lemma 1 she knows ${\uparrow}(0,2)$: that the joint state is at least as probable as it actually is. That is ordinary cliff-edge knowledge, and it stands or falls with MARGINS. The I-probabilist's exclusion of $(2,2)$ is licensed by INDUCTIVE KNOWLEDGE. Actuality is sufficiently more normal than $(2,2)$ in that ordering, because double failure is improbable in aggregate.

In K's exclusion on products, neither licence is present. $(0,2) \not\gg (1,2)$, and the agent lacks cliff-edge knowledge. So SEPARATION divides the multi-dimensionalist from the probabilist's joint contexts. But it isolates something in K on products that neither the probabilist's aggregate knowledge nor ordinary cliff-edge knowledge shares. A question that singles out $(1,2)$ would exclude it under any completion. That is one reason Goodman and Salow want a "reasonably constrained" account of questions (117).

## 7. Meet-normality

Can a theorist who wants cliff-edge knowledge and KK respect SEPARATION? Define

$$\textsc{Meet}:\ R_K(w) = {\uparrow}w \cup \{v \in E : \exists b \in R_B\ \ v \succeq w \wedge b\},$$

where $w \wedge b$ is a greatest lower bound of $w$ and $b$ under $\succeq$. Which one is chosen does not matter, since equivalent bounds have the same upper set. The definition uses only the global relations. The ${\uparrow}w$ term secures FACTIVITY when $R_B$ is empty.

**Proposition 5.** (a) Given COMPARABILITY, meet-normality coincides with K-NORMALITY. On multi-dimensional structures:

(b) meet-normality gives $\prod_d (B_d \cup {\uparrow}w_d)$ if $R_B \neq \emptyset$, and ${\uparrow}w$ otherwise;

(c) it satisfies the framework's five principles and SEPARATION, and its accessibility relation is transitive, so it validates KK given TRANSPARENCY;

(d) if $R_B \neq \emptyset$, it is the most restrictive relation compatible with FACTIVITY, INDUCTIVE LIMITS, CONVEXITY and COMBINATION.

*Proof.* (a) Under COMPARABILITY, $w \wedge b$ is the less normal of $w$ and $b$. If $R_B = \emptyset$, both completions give ${\uparrow}w$. If $w \in R_B$, every bound $w \wedge b$ lies in the upward-closed set $R_B$, and $b$ lies above it, so the union is $R_B$. If $w \notin R_B$, then $R_B \subseteq {\uparrow}w$ by Lemma 1, so $w \wedge b$ is equivalent to $w$ and the union is ${\uparrow}w$.

(b) Meets are taken respect by respect. It suffices to show that $v_d \succeq_d \min(w_d, b_d)$ for some $b_d \in B_d$ iff $v_d \in B_d \cup {\uparrow}w_d$. If the minimum is $w_d$, then $v_d \in {\uparrow}w_d$; if it is $b_d$, then $v_d \in B_d$ by upward closure. Conversely, take $b_d = v_d$ when $v_d \in B_d$.

(c) INDUCTIVE KNOWLEDGE: if $w \gg v$, then some $d$ has $w_d \gg_d v_d$. So $v_d \notin B_d$, and $v_d \notin {\uparrow}w_d$. INDUCTIVE LIMITS: $R_B = \prod_d B_d$. CONVEXITY: a product of upward-closed sets is upward closed. SEPARATION: a product containing $w$ satisfies COMBINATION. Transitivity holds respect by respect: if $v_d \in {\uparrow}w_d$, then ${\uparrow}v_d \subseteq {\uparrow}w_d$, and if $v_d \in B_d$, then ${\uparrow}v_d \subseteq B_d$.

(d) Let $R$ satisfy the four principles. Then $R \supseteq R_B \cup \{w\}$. Let $U$ be the set of respects in which $w$ is outside belief. If $U$ is empty, meet-normality gives $R_B$. Otherwise it gives the product of ${\uparrow}w_f$ for $f \in U$ and $B_f$ for $f \notin U$, by nesting. Take $v$ in this product, and choose $x \in R_B$ that agrees with $v$ outside $U$. COMBINATION puts $w_U x$ in $R$. Then $v \succeq w_U x$, so CONVEXITY puts $v$ in $R$. ∎

Three corollaries are immediate on products. $R_K$ grows in the order K, meet, KM, I. Meet-normality's set is the smallest product containing K-NORMALITY's. And the two agree on every single-respect proposition.

Meet-normality is thus what Goodman and Salow's own rationale for K yields once SEPARATION is respected. It is the knowledge default given SEPARATION, as K is the knowledge default without it. Its knowledge outside belief is, respect by respect, either true belief or cliff-edge knowledge about the failing respect. It violates MARGINS: at $(1,1)$ the agent knows that neither respect is bizarre, while under KM she knows nothing.

The alternative completions that Goodman and Salow reject in n. 18 aim at MARGINS and fail CONVEXITY or INDUCTIVE LIMITS (108). Meet-normality rejects MARGINS and satisfies all five principles, so it competes with K rather than with them.

There are two limits. First, meet-normality needs greatest lower bounds. Discovery in Goodman and Salow's sense intersects the evidential cell with the discovered proposition (124–125). Discovering a proposition about one respect leaves a product. Discovering a proposition that links respects can leave a cell without meets. If the agent in figure 4 discovers that $(1,1)$ does not obtain, $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ have no greatest lower bound in the new cell. There, the knowledge-default characterization in (d) is the natural general definition, but I have not investigated its logic. Second, a theorist could instead keep K-NORMALITY together with COMPARABILITY, answering the orthogonality argument as the probabilist does. She then accepts the joint-context verdicts of §6.

## 8. Consequences

**The abductive link.** Goodman and Salow's second step is this: if COMPARABILITY fails, the MARGINS question is K against KM, and K is simpler (111). The standard of simplicity they apply to I-NORMALITY is being "encouragingly simple, non-disjunctive, and parsimonious, featuring only one relation of comparative normality" (111). By that standard, Theorem 2 reverses the comparison on the structures that motivate rejecting COMPARABILITY. There KM-NORMALITY is non-disjunctive and uses only $\gg$, while K-NORMALITY's disjunction is active.

One might reply that simplicity attaches to general definitions rather than to their restrictions. But the step is conditional on COMPARABILITY's failing, and failing in the multi-dimensional way that Goodman and Salow find most compelling (109). What matters is which theory gives the simpler account of those structures. In addition, §5 shows that K-NORMALITY incurs a Gettier cost there which KM-NORMALITY does not. So the step "if COMPARABILITY fails, MARGINS fails" loses both supports for multi-dimensional incomparability. This does not refute the hedged suggestion. It removes one of its stated grounds.

If SEPARATION is accepted, more follows. The live choice on products is between KM- and meet-normality. By Theorem 2 and Proposition 5(b), within each respect these are I- and K-NORMALITY, where COMPARABILITY holds. So for incomparability of this kind, the MARGINS question is the question it would be under COMPARABILITY, asked once for each respect.

**KK.** Defenders of KK who rely on K-NORMALITY (see Goodman and Salow 2023, 107 n. 16) and accept the orthogonality argument face a choice. They can keep K and accept *Thermometer*. They can move to meet-normality, which agrees with K wherever COMPARABILITY holds and validates KK under TRANSPARENCY. Or they can keep COMPARABILITY with the probabilist. None of these choices decides KK, whose status in the framework turns on further issues that Goodman and Salow leave open (107 and n. 17).

**The probabilist.** In a context whose question concerns respect $d$ alone, Goodman and Salow's probabilist compares situations by the probability of their state in $d$. Their sufficiency proposal (121) then yields relations on $X_d$ that satisfy (i)–(iii).

**Corollary 6.** Suppose each respect's relations are the probabilist's single-respect relations. Then at every situation, KM-NORMALITY's accessibility set is the intersection of the I-NORMALITY sets of the single-respect contexts. Meet-normality's set is the intersection of their K-NORMALITY sets.

*Proof.* Each context is comparable. In context $d$, I-NORMALITY leaves open $\{v : w_d \not\gg_d v_d\}$, and K-NORMALITY leaves open $\{v : v_d \in B_d \cup {\uparrow}w_d\}$. Now apply Theorem 2 and Proposition 5(b). ∎

This sharpens Goodman and Salow's context-by-context reply (121). It also sharpens their location of the dispute at the preface (122–123). The multi-dimensionalist and the probabilist agree on every single-respect question, whichever completion they share. They part over two things: whether knowledge agglomerates across respects, and whether joint contexts yield knowledge that links respects. SEPARATION bears on the second.

## 9. Objections and replies

### 9.1 The knowledge is just the price of the knowledge default

*Objection.* K-NORMALITY is principled: it adds to belief only what FACTIVITY and CONVEXITY force (2023, 104, 110). The verdict in *Thermometer* is what that rationale yields when belief and ${\uparrow}w$ do not nest, and a K-theorist may accept it.

*Reply.* She may. The cost is now exact. She must accept, at the single-malfunction situations of the model that motivates rejecting COMPARABILITY, the verdict of the account that Goodman and Salow reject as Gettier-prone. She must also give up the reply to Williamson for her own completion. And meet-normality removes the cost without giving up the knowledge default's spirit, any cliff-edge knowledge, or KK. What remains of the objection is a question about method: whether minimality, unrestricted, should outrank verdicts about Gettier cases. Minimality alone cannot settle that within the framework, since JTB-NORMALITY is also a minimal default (113), and Goodman and Salow reject it for its Gettier verdicts (113–114).

### 9.2 Respects are not part of the framework

*Objection.* The framework characterizes knowledge from $\succeq$ and $\gg$ alone (2023, 97). SEPARATION adverts to respects.

*Reply.* The multi-dimensional account defines $\succeq$ and $\gg$ from respects, and the orthogonality argument is stated in terms of them. Lemma 1, Theorem 2 and the definition of meet-normality use only the global relations. Respects enter only in describing which disjunctions are Gettier disjunctions, and Gettier cases are individuated by subject matter in any case.

### 9.3 The probabilist refutes SEPARATION

*Objection.* In the joint context the probabilist's agent knows that the instruments have not both failed grossly, in violation of SEPARATION (§6). If that knowledge is genuine, SEPARATION is false.

*Reply.* If the probabilist's joint contexts are admissible and their verdicts genuine, SEPARATION needs restriction to knowledge not licensed by INDUCTIVE KNOWLEDGE. Since INDUCTIVE KNOWLEDGE is a framework principle, that restriction is not ad hoc. The restricted principle still condemns *Thermometer*, where actuality is not sufficiently more normal than the excluded $(1,2)$ in the product ordering, in either single-respect ordering, or in the joint ordering. It is also still met by KM- and meet-normality on products. Whether joint contexts are admissible is part of the open question Goodman and Salow leave about constraining questions (117).

## 10. Conclusion

On Goodman and Salow's multi-dimensional account, K-NORMALITY respects the independence their anti-skeptical argument demands, but it does something that argument does not address. When one source fails beyond belief and another works well, it credits knowledge of a disjunction of a justified false belief about one source and an unknown truth about the other. This is not cliff-edge knowledge. At the situations the orthogonality argument concerns, it is what justified-true-belief normality says.

CONVEXITY does not exclude this knowledge, so Goodman and Salow's reply to the disjunctive diagnosis of Gettier cases protects KM-NORMALITY, which is non-disjunctive on these structures, and not K-NORMALITY. The principle that bans such knowledge is equivalent to combination across respects. With it, K-NORMALITY's knowledge default becomes meet-normality, which keeps KK. What has not been shown is that SEPARATION holds beyond independent subject matters, or how the KK-friendly completion should behave on evidential cells without meets.

Every formal claim is proved above. The finite claims were also checked by exhaustive enumeration with a script, over 5,949 multi-dimensional structures and 3,594 comparable ones. Proposition 4 was checked by brute force over all disjunctions on a sample of 310 structures. The infinite cases (integer and natural-number respects, including one with empty $R_B$) were checked with the z3 solver.
