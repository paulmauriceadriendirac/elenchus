# What Global Accuracy Arguments for Conditionalization Assume About How We Learn

**Abstract.** The classic expected-accuracy argument for conditionalization assumes that evidence comes from a known partition. Without that assumption, the accuracy-maximizing plan conditions on the fact that one received one's evidence rather than on its content. A recent reply evaluates update rules globally: among rules whose output depends only on the content learned, conditionalization maximizes expected accuracy summed over learning situations. I prove that for any weighting of learning situations, the optimal such rule conditions the prior on the proposition that one's evidence is $E$. The partition-free result and the global result are both special cases. Conditionalization on $E$ is optimal exactly when the weighting makes every $E$-world equally likely to yield $E$. This condition fails for almost every weighting, and it fails for every agent certain that her evidence is non-partitional. The global argument therefore rests on a symmetry assumption about learning, not on a constraint on which rules are available.

## 1. Introduction

The best-known accuracy argument for conditionalization is a theorem of Greaves and Wallace (2006). An agent with a probabilistic prior expects to learn which cell of a known partition is true. Among the plans she could adopt that respond only to what she learns, the plans that maximize her expected accuracy are exactly the conditionalizing ones. The theorem has been extended to infinite settings (Easwaran 2013), and it sits at the centre of accuracy-first epistemology (Joyce 1998; Pettigrew 2016). Its partition assumption has proved to be its weak point. If what one learns in a world need not be what one would learn in every world compatible with it, as externalists about evidence hold, then the optimal plan conditions not on the proposition learned but on the fact that one learned it (Schoenfield 2017). A line of work has since asked what this means for updating: whether accuracy vindications of updating plans can be squared with evidence externalism (Das 2019), whether agents whose beliefs do not discriminate perfectly between situations should conditionalize at all (Isaacs and Russell 2023), and how the results extend to infinite non-partitional settings (Easwaran and Nielsen 2025).

Meehan and Zhang (2025) defend conditionalization by changing what is evaluated. Instead of an agent's plan for one learning situation, they evaluate a rule that is applied across many. They restrict attention to rules that are *globally evidentially constant*: if two situations deliver the same evidence, the rule delivers the same posterior. Among such rules, they argue, conditionalization maximizes total expected accuracy across learning situations, including non-transparent ones. A natural moral is that the disagreement between conditionalization and its externalist rivals reduces to a question of availability: can an agent's update depend on features of her learning situation beyond the content of what she learned, or only on that content?

I argue that this moral is mistaken, and I say exactly what the global argument needs instead. The thesis is this:

> Among globally evidentially constant rules, the expected-accuracy-optimal rule for *any* weighting of learning situations conditions the prior on the proposition that one's evidence is $E$; it coincides with conditionalization on $E$ if and only if the weighting is *content-sufficient*, that is, it makes every $E$-world equally likely to yield exactly $E$.

The thesis has three consequences. First, evidential constancy does not by itself favour conditionalization. Whether the optimal constant rule is conditionalization is settled entirely by the weighting, and the externalist rule that conditions on the fact of learning is itself evidentially constant once the weighting is read as the agent's credence. Second, the summed total that delivers conditionalization is a content-sufficient weighting, and its content-sufficiency comes from a symmetry among worlds. Third, content-sufficient weightings are non-generic, and they exclude every agent who is certain that her evidence function is non-partitional. They do not exclude every agent who is certain that her evidence is non-transparent. A case of two "cyclic" informants shows that content-sufficiency, not partitionality, is the condition that matters.

The increment over prior work is precise. Schoenfield's theorem, the Greaves–Wallace theorem and the global theorem (as I regiment it in §2) all follow as corollaries of one characterization (Theorem 1), and the characterization says which weightings of learning situations favour conditionalization (Theorem 2). The condition has a long history in statistics, where its analogue for incomplete data is called *coarsening at random*. As far as I know, its role as the hidden premise of the global argument has not been identified. I also show that the global argument cannot be rescued by reading it as a worst-case argument (Proposition 4): conditionalization is not minimax-optimal among evidentially constant rules.

The thesis is not that conditionalization is wrong or that Meehan and Zhang's theorem is false. It is that the theorem's conclusion is driven by a premise about how one learns, which an agent's credences can violate and, in the cases that motivated the externalist challenge, do violate. My results concern finite sets of worlds and factive evidence unless I say otherwise. Since I have regimented the global argument in a particular way, I state in §6 why the results survive other regimentations.

For readers outside formal epistemology the point can be put simply. When we learn something, we learn it in a particular way, and the way can itself be informative. A witness who would have told you more if things were otherwise has told you something by telling you less. Conditionalizing on what you learned ignores this. The global argument promised to show that, evaluated as a policy across all the ways one might learn, ignoring it is best. The result below shows that the promise holds only if the ways one might learn are weighted so that, on balance, they carry no such information.

Section 2 sets out the framework and the three known results. Section 3 proves the main theorem. Section 4 characterizes content-sufficiency and gives the cases. Section 5 states the dilemma about weights that the theorem forces on the global argument. Section 6 replies to objections.

## 2. Framework

Let $W$ be a finite set of worlds with at least two members, and let $p$ be a probability function on the subsets of $W$. I assume $p$ is *regular*: $p(w) > 0$ for every $w$. A *credence function* assigns numbers in $[0,1]$ to subsets of $W$. An *inaccuracy measure* $I(c, w)$ scores credence function $c$ at world $w$. It is *strictly proper* if, for every probability function $q$, the $q$-expected inaccuracy $\sum_w q(w)\, I(c,w)$ is uniquely minimized at $c = q$. The Brier score and the logarithmic score are examples. I assume nothing else about $I$: neither additivity nor continuity is used below.

An *evidence function* $f$ maps each world to a non-empty set of worlds, the proposition the agent learns there. It is *factive* if $w \in f(w)$ for every $w$. It is *partitional* if it is factive and $w' \in f(w)$ implies $f(w') = f(w)$. Partitional functions model learning in which the agent can always tell what she has learned; non-partitional factive functions model the externalist's cases, where what one learns in a good case can be stronger than what one learns in a bad case that is indistinguishable from it. I call an evidence function a *learning situation*.

For a single learning situation $f$, a *plan* maps worlds to credence functions, and a plan is *available* if it assigns the same credence function to any two worlds at which $f$ delivers the same proposition. Two results concern plans.

**(GW)** If $f$ is partitional, the available plans that maximize $p$-expected accuracy are exactly those that conditionalize on $f(w)$ (Greaves and Wallace 2006).

**(S)** For arbitrary $f$, let $f^*(w) = \{w' : f(w') = f(w)\}$, the proposition that one's evidence is $f(w)$. The available plans that maximize $p$-expected accuracy are those that conditionalize on $f^*(w)$ (Schoenfield 2017).

When $f$ is partitional, $f^*(w) = f(w)$, so (S) generalizes (GW). When it is not, the two come apart. Suppose that in world $a$ one learns $\{a\}$ and in world $b$ one learns $\{a,b\}$. Then learning $\{a,b\}$ happens only in $b$. The $f^*$-conditionalizer becomes certain of $b$, while the conditionalizer on $\{a,b\}$ divides her credence between $a$ and $b$.

A *global rule* $R$ assigns a credence function $R(E)$ to each non-empty proposition $E$. Applied in learning situation $f$, it yields the plan $w \mapsto R(f(w))$. A rule that receives the evidence and the situation could have posteriors that vary with the situation. *Global evidential constancy* forbids this: if $f(w) = f'(w')$ then the posterior in $(f, w)$ equals the posterior in $(f', w')$. I treat global rules as functions of the content learned. They are then evidentially constant by construction, and every evidentially constant rule is one. *Conditionalization* is the rule $C(E) = p(\cdot \mid E)$.

To evaluate a global rule we need a way of combining its performance across situations. Let $\Phi$ be a finite set of learning situations and let $P$ be a probability function on $W \times \Phi$ whose marginal on $W$ is $p$. Then the *weighted expected inaccuracy* of $R$ is

$$T_P(R) = \sum_{(w,f)} P(w,f)\, I\big(R(f(w)), w\big).$$

In the simplest case the weighting is independent of the world, $P = p \times \lambda$ for a probability function $\lambda$ on $\Phi$. Meehan and Zhang's total sums expected inaccuracy over all factive evidence functions on $W$ with no weights, which up to a constant factor is the case where $\lambda$ is uniform on the set $\Phi_{\mathrm{fact}}$ of all factive evidence functions. This yields my regimentation of the global theorem:

**(MZ)** If $P = p \times \mathrm{unif}(\Phi_{\mathrm{fact}})$, conditionalization is the unique global rule that minimizes $T_P$ (Meehan and Zhang 2025).

The regimentation follows the published description of the result. Its proof here is a corollary of Theorem 1, so nothing below depends on the details of their proof. Section 6 takes up the worry that the regimentation is unfaithful.

## 3. The weighted theorem

Define the *learning likelihood* of $E$ at $w$ under $P$ as the probability, given $w$, that one learns exactly $E$:

$$\ell_P(E \mid w) = P\big(\{f : f(w) = E\} \mid w\big).$$

Let $m_P(E) = \sum_w p(w)\, \ell_P(E \mid w)$ be the probability of learning $E$. When $m_P(E) > 0$, define the probability function

$$q_E(w) = \frac{p(w)\, \ell_P(E \mid w)}{m_P(E)}.$$

By Bayes's theorem, $q_E$ is the marginal on $W$ of $P$ conditioned on the proposition $\{(w,f) : f(w) = E\}$, that is, on the proposition *that one's evidence is $E$*.

**Theorem 1.** A global rule $R$ minimizes $T_P$ if and only if $R(E) = q_E$ for every $E$ with $m_P(E) > 0$.

*Proof.* Group the terms of $T_P(R)$ by the proposition learned:
$$T_P(R) = \sum_{E} \sum_{w} p(w)\, \ell_P(E\mid w)\, I(R(E), w) = \sum_{E:\, m_P(E)>0} m_P(E) \sum_w q_E(w)\, I(R(E), w).$$
The value $R(E)$ enters only the $E$-th summand, so $T_P$ is minimized just in case each summand is. The $E$-th summand is $m_P(E)$ times the $q_E$-expected inaccuracy of $R(E)$, which by strict propriety is uniquely minimized at $R(E) = q_E$. Propositions with $m_P(E) = 0$ contribute nothing, so $R$ is unconstrained there. ∎

The proof is short because the evaluation is separable: once the rule is required to depend only on content, the total breaks into one decision problem per content, and each is settled by propriety. What the proof shows is what each decision problem is about. The weight on world $w$ in the problem for $E$ is not $p(w)$ restricted to $E$. It is $p(w)$ multiplied by the probability of learning $E$ there. I checked the theorem numerically for $|W| = 3$ with the Brier, spherical and logarithmic scores: for 200 random pairs of a prior and a weighting over the 64 factive evidence functions, no perturbation of the rule $E \mapsto q_E$ lowered $T_P$.

Four corollaries locate the known results.

**Corollary 1 (Schoenfield).** If $P = p \times \delta_f$, a point mass on one situation $f$, then $q_E = p(\cdot \mid \{w : f(w) = E\})$, so the optimal rule conditionalizes on $f^*$.

This is immediate, since $\ell_P(E \mid w)$ is 1 if $f(w) = E$ and 0 otherwise. The available plans for $f$ correspond one-to-one to global rules restricted to the contents $f$ can deliver, so Corollary 1 is (S). The rule $E \mapsto p(\cdot \mid \{w : f(w)=E\})$ is a global rule. It takes the content learned as its only input, as conditionalization does. For an agent certain of her learning situation, the rule (S) recommends is therefore evidentially constant. Evidential constancy alone does not separate (S) from (MZ).

**Corollary 2 (Greaves–Wallace).** If $P = p \times \delta_f$ and $f$ is partitional, the optimal rule is conditionalization.

**Corollary 3 (the global theorem).** Let $\lambda$ be a probability function on a set $\Phi$ of factive evidence functions, and suppose $\lambda$ is invariant under relabelling worlds: for every permutation $\sigma$ of $W$, $\Phi$ is closed under $f \mapsto \sigma f \sigma^{-1}$ and $\lambda$ gives $f$ and $\sigma f \sigma^{-1}$ equal weight. Then for $P = p\times\lambda$ the optimal rule is conditionalization. In particular (MZ) holds.

*Proof.* Let $w, w' \in E$ and let $\sigma$ swap them, so $\sigma E = E$. Then $f(w) = E$ iff $(\sigma f \sigma^{-1})(w') = E$, and the map $f \mapsto \sigma f\sigma^{-1}$ preserves $\lambda$. Hence $\ell_P(E \mid w) = \ell_P(E\mid w')$ for all $w,w' \in E$, and Theorem 2 below applies. For the uniform weighting on $\Phi_{\mathrm{fact}}$, a direct count gives $\ell_P(E \mid w) = 2^{-(|W|-1)}$ for every $w \in E$. ∎

**Corollary 4 (non-factive situations).** If $\lambda$ is uniform on the set of *all* evidence functions on $W$, factive or not, then $q_E = p$ for every $E$: the optimal rule ignores the evidence.

*Proof.* The number of functions with $f(w) = E$ is $(2^{|W|}-1)^{|W|-1}$, whatever $w$ and $E$ are. So $\ell_P(E\mid \cdot)$ is constant on all of $W$, and $q_E = p$. ∎

It has been observed that the global result does not survive the inclusion of non-factive evidence functions. Corollary 4 says exactly what happens then. The uniform total then makes learning $E$ equally likely at every world, inside or outside $E$, and the best constant rule does not learn at all. This shows how much of the global argument's verdict comes from the choice of family and weights. Over factive situations the uniform weighting yields conditionalization; over all situations the same weighting yields no updating.

The four corollaries were checked by exact rational arithmetic for $|W|=3$ (64 factive and 343 total evidence functions).

## 4. Content-sufficiency

**Definition.** $P$ is *content-sufficient* if for every $E$ and all $w, w' \in E$, $\ell_P(E\mid w) = \ell_P(E\mid w')$.

Content-sufficiency says that learning exactly $E$, as opposed to some other truth, carries no information about which $E$-world one is in beyond the information that $E$ is true. The fact of learning adds nothing to the content learned.

**Theorem 2.** Let $p$ be regular and let all situations in the support of $P$ be factive. Conditionalization minimizes $T_P$ (and is then the unique minimizer on every $E$ with $m_P(E) > 0$) if and only if $P$ is content-sufficient on every such $E$.

*Proof.* By Theorem 1, conditionalization is optimal iff $q_E = p(\cdot\mid E)$ whenever $m_P(E)>0$. By factivity $\ell_P(E\mid w) = 0$ for $w \notin E$, so both functions vanish outside $E$. For $w \in E$, $q_E(w) = p(w)\ell_P(E\mid w)/m_P(E)$ and $p(w \mid E) = p(w)/p(E)$. Since $p(w) > 0$, these agree for all $w\in E$ iff $\ell_P(E\mid w) = m_P(E)/p(E)$ for all $w \in E$, that is, iff $\ell_P(E\mid\cdot)$ is constant on $E$. ∎

Theorem 2 makes the question of whether conditionalization is optimal answerable by inspecting the learning likelihoods. Four facts about content-sufficiency follow. Together they show where the global argument's verdict comes from.

**Proposition 3.** Let $P = p \times \lambda$ with $\lambda$ on factive situations.

(a) If $\lambda$ is a point mass on $f$, then $P$ is content-sufficient iff $f$ is partitional.

(b) If every situation in the support of $\lambda$ is partitional, $P$ is content-sufficient, whatever the weights.

(c) $P$ can be content-sufficient even when no situation in the support of $\lambda$ is partitional.

(d) The weightings $\lambda$ on $\Phi_{\mathrm{fact}}$ for which $P$ is content-sufficient form a set of Lebesgue measure zero in the simplex of all such weightings.

*Proof.* (a) With a point mass, $\ell(E\mid w) = 1$ if $f(w)=E$ and 0 otherwise. Constancy on $E = f(w)$ requires $f(w') = E$ for every $w' \in f(w)$, which is partitionality. (b) If $f$ is partitional and $w \in E$, then $f(w) = E$ iff $E$ is a cell of $f$. This condition does not mention $w$. So $\ell(E\mid w) = \lambda(\{f : E \text{ is a cell of } f\})$ for every $w\in E$. (c) See the cyclic case below. (d) Each content-sufficiency constraint $\ell(E\mid w) - \ell(E \mid w') = 0$ is linear in $\lambda$. The constraint is not satisfied identically on the simplex: the point mass on the situation that sends $w$ to $\{w\}$, $w'$ to $\{w,w'\}$ and every other world $v$ to $\{v\}$ violates it for $E = \{w,w'\}$. So the content-sufficient weightings lie in a proper affine subspace of the simplex's affine hull, which has measure zero. ∎

Clause (a) recovers the partitional/non-partitional boundary for an agent certain of her situation. Clause (b) extends (GW): an agent certain only that her evidence will be partitional, and uncertain which partition, should still conditionalize. Clauses (c) and (d) show that the boundary drawn by (GW) and (S) is the wrong one in general. Here is the case behind (c).

> *Two Cyclic Informants.* There are three worlds, 1, 2 and 3. An informant will tell you a true disjunction of two worlds. Informant A, in world 1, says "1 or 2"; in world 2, "2 or 3"; in world 3, "3 or 1". Informant B goes the other way: "1 or 3" in world 1, "2 or 1" in world 2, "3 or 2" in world 3. You do not know which informant you will meet, and your credence is $1/2$ in each.

Each informant is a non-partitional, indeed non-transparent, learning situation. If you knew which informant you faced, each report would identify the world: A says "1 or 2" only in world 1. You are certain that your evidence will come from a non-partitional situation. Yet each report is equally likely in both of its worlds: "1 or 2" has probability $1/2$ in world 1 (from A) and $1/2$ in world 2 (from B). So the weighting is content-sufficient, and by Theorem 2 conditionalization is the optimal constant rule. A transparency or partitionality condition would classify the case wrongly. In terms of what learning reveals, the situations average out into something that behaves like a partition. I verified the likelihoods and the coincidence with conditionalization by exact arithmetic for a non-uniform prior.

Clause (d) shows that this kind of averaging is fragile. The next case shows what happens when it fails.

> *Two Instruments.* There are three equiprobable worlds, $a$, $b$ and $c$. You will read one of two instruments, with credence $1/2$ in each. The coarse instrument reports $\{a,b\}$ in $a$ and in $b$, and $\{c\}$ in $c$. The fine instrument is sharper in the good case: it reports $\{a\}$ in $a$, $\{a,b\}$ in $b$, and $\{c\}$ in $c$. The instrument reports $\{a,b\}$.

The fine instrument has the structure that motivated the externalist challenge. In the good case $a$ you learn more than in the bad case $b$, though $b$ is compatible with what you learn in $a$. Here $\ell(\{a,b\}\mid a) = 1/2$, since only the coarse instrument reports $\{a,b\}$ in $a$, and $\ell(\{a,b\}\mid b) = 1$, since both do in $b$. Theorem 1 gives $q_{\{a,b\}} = (1/3, 2/3, 0)$, while conditionalization gives $(1/2, 1/2, 0)$. With the Brier score summed over the three atoms, the agent's expected inaccuracy is $2/9$ for the $q$-rule and $1/4$ for conditionalization. Both rules are evidentially constant, and they take the same input. The agent should expect the rule that is not conditionalization to do better. The intuitive reason is that hearing $\{a,b\}$ is some evidence that one is not in the world where the fine instrument would have said more.

## 5. Whose weights?

Theorem 1 shows that the global argument's conclusion depends on how learning situations are weighted. It does not depend on the restriction to evidentially constant rules. So the argument needs an account of the weights. There are two readings, and each leaves conditionalization with a narrower vindication than the global argument claims.

**The credal reading.** The weights are the agent's credences about which learning situation she is in, possibly correlated with the world. This is the reading that fits the argument's form. An expected-accuracy argument tells an agent which option she should expect to do best, and expectation is taken by her credences. On this reading Theorem 1 is a vindication of conditionalization, but of conditionalization of her joint prior on the proposition that her evidence is $E$. Conditionalization on the content $E$ is vindicated just when her credences about how she learns are content-sufficient (Theorem 2). An agent certain of a non-partitional situation is never in that position (Proposition 3a). The agents who prompted the externalist challenge are of this kind: they know that their evidence is stronger in good cases than in bad ones. So the credal reading returns Schoenfield's verdict on Schoenfield's cases (Corollary 1), and it returns conditionalization on content exactly where the fact of learning is uninformative.

**The evaluative reading.** The weights are not the agent's credences but a standard for assessing rules: a rule is better if it does better summed over the situations, whatever the agent thinks about which one she faces. The uniform total in (MZ) is naturally read this way. Theorem 1 then shows what this standard comes to. It is content-sufficient because it is symmetric under relabelling worlds (Corollary 3). It treats every world as equally liable to yield each true proposition. That is a substantive assumption about the learning environment. Two things follow.

First, an agent who has credences about her situation that are not content-sufficient, and who adopts conditionalization because it is best by the evaluative standard, adopts a rule she expects to be less accurate than an available alternative. In *Two Instruments* she does so by $1/36$ in Brier terms. By the uniqueness clause of Theorem 1, the same holds for every weighting that is not content-sufficient. The expected-accuracy arguments for updating norms (Greaves and Wallace 2006; Pettigrew 2016) recommend what the agent should expect to do best. A standard on which the agent expects the recommended rule to do worse departs from that methodology. It can be defended, but the defence must come from outside expected accuracy.

Second, the evaluative standard is fragile in its own terms. Corollary 4 shows that the same impartial weighting over a slightly larger family recommends not updating at all. What favours conditionalization is the conjunction of factivity with symmetry, not impartiality as such. Impartiality over factive situations is one content-sufficient weighting among many, and content-sufficient weightings are non-generic (Proposition 3d).

**Availability.** The dilemma also shows why the dispute does not reduce to availability. Suppose availability means that an update may depend on what was learned and not on further features of the situation that the agent cannot detect. Then every rule $E \mapsto q_E$ is available, because it takes the content as its only input. Running it requires no more at the time of learning than conditionalization requires, and it requires a prior over $W\times\Phi$ rather than over $W$. The externalist's rule, which conditions on the fact of learning, does not need the agent to detect which situation she is in. It needs her to have had credences about how she would learn, and those credences enter through the prior. If the objection is that she cannot have such credences, it is an objection to a prior. And the uniform weighting is itself a prior of that kind: it is a credence over situations, or else it is an evaluative standard, which brings us back to the dilemma.

## 6. Objections and replies

**Objection 1: Impartiality is the point.** A rule is meant to be good as a rule, across all the situations in which it might be used. It is not meant to be good for one agent's guess about her situation. So the evaluative reading is the intended one, and the agent's credences about her situation are beside the point.

*Reply.* I accept that this yields a coherent standard, and that conditionalization is the unique best rule by it. The cost is the one identified in §5: the standard can recommend a rule that the agent who uses it expects to be worse. There is also a question about what impartiality requires. Impartiality between situations is not the same as symmetry between worlds. The uniform weighting over factive situations satisfies both, but Theorem 1 shows that only the second matters to the verdict. A weighting that treats situations evenly within some natural class that is not closed under relabelling worlds, such as the situations produced by a fixed instrument in different calibrations, need not be content-sufficient. The defender of the evaluative reading must therefore defend symmetry among worlds with respect to how they are learned about. Where rules are designed for a population of agents spread across situations, there may be such a defence. The reply succeeds for that use. It does not show that an individual agent who knows something about her situation should conditionalize.

**Objection 2: Read the argument as a worst-case argument.** The global argument shows that conditionalization does well whatever situation one is in, and does not depend on weights at all.

*Reply.* This reading does not support conditionalization.

**Proposition 4.** Let $V(R) = \max_{f \in \Phi_{\mathrm{fact}}} \sum_w p(w)\, I(R(f(w)),w)$ be the worst-case expected inaccuracy of a global rule over factive situations. The rule $R_0(E) = p$, which never updates, minimizes $V$. Conditionalization does not minimize $V$ in general.

*Proof.* Because a factive situation chooses $f(w)$ independently for each $w$, $V(R) = \sum_w p(w) \max_{E \ni w} I(R(E), w) \ge \sum_w p(w)\, I(R(W), w) \ge \sum_w p(w)\, I(p,w)$. The last inequality is propriety. $R_0$ attains the bound. For the second claim, take the Brier score summed over atoms, $W = \{1,2,3\}$ and $p = (1/10, 9/20, 9/20)$. Conditionalizing on $\{1,2\}$ gives $(2/11, 9/11, 0)$, whose inaccuracy at world 1 is $162/121$, more than the inaccuracy $243/200$ of $p$ itself. Then $V(C) = 144567/242000 \approx 0.597$, whereas $V(R_0) = 117/200 = 0.585$. ∎

The worst case is the case of misleadingly selective evidence: a true proposition that excludes worlds in which one's credence was low and keeps the one world one was confident was false. Against an adversarial choice of situation, refusing to learn is optimal. So the worst-case reading cannot rescue conditionalization, and the global argument needs an expectation and hence weights.

**Objection 3: Indifference.** Absent specific information about how she will learn, an agent should spread her credence evenly over situations. By Corollary 3 that makes her content-sufficient. So conditionalization is the right default.

*Reply.* I accept the conditional: an agent whose credences about situations are invariant under relabelling worlds should conditionalize. This is a real vindication, and it is the strongest conclusion the global argument supports. Three limits apply. First, the vindication is local to such agents. The cases that prompted the externalist challenge are cases in which the agent knows the shape of her evidence. She knows that she learns more in good cases, and that knowledge breaks the invariance (Proposition 3a). Second, invariance is not the only way to be content-sufficient. *Two Cyclic Informants* shows that an agent with specific, asymmetric information about her situation can be content-sufficient too. The right default is therefore content-sufficiency, not ignorance. Third, the vindication is not stable under small changes of information, though its practical cost is. Since $q_E$ varies continuously with $P$, an agent whose credences are nearly content-sufficient loses little by conditionalizing. But she still does better with $q$, by Theorem 1. Conditionalization is then a good approximation, which is a weaker claim than that it is the uniquely rational rule.

**Objection 4: The rule $q$ is conditionalization.** Theorem 1 says that the optimal rule conditionalizes the joint prior on the agent's total evidence, including the fact that she learned $E$. That is Bayesianism vindicated, not refuted.

*Reply.* I agree with the first sentence, and it is part of what I want to establish. The theorem gives a unified expected-accuracy vindication of conditionalization *on the proposition that one's evidence is $E$*, over a space rich enough to contain it. But the disagreement between (S) and (MZ) was never about whether some conditionalization is optimal. It was about whether conditionalizing on the content $E$, the proposition that becomes certain, is optimal. On that question the answer is "iff content-sufficient". In non-transparent cases the agent's posterior over worlds should differ from $p(\cdot \mid E)$, as in *Two Instruments*, where it should be $(1/3, 2/3, 0)$ rather than $(1/2, 1/2, 0)$. Calling both rules "conditionalization" relabels the dispute without settling it.

**Objection 5: The regimentation is unfaithful.** Meehan and Zhang's family of situations, their weighting or their notion of a rule may differ from (MZ).

*Reply.* Theorem 1 holds for every finite family and every weighting, including weightings correlated with the world. Whatever family and weights the global argument uses, its verdict is conditionalization iff they are content-sufficient. If the family is closed under relabelling worlds and the weights are equal, Corollary 3 applies and the verdict is conditionalization. If not, Theorem 2 says what must be checked. The one regimentation choice that matters is that rules are scored by an expectation over situations. Objection 2 shows that the main alternative to expectation does not favour conditionalization. My results do not cover infinite settings, where the decomposition in the proof of Theorem 1 needs measure-theoretic care that I have not supplied. Signal-based frameworks for infinite non-partitional settings (Easwaran and Nielsen 2025) are the natural place to test an extension.

## 7. Conclusion

I have proved that, among evidentially constant update rules, the expected-accuracy-optimal rule for any weighting of learning situations conditions the prior on the proposition that one's evidence is $E$. Schoenfield's theorem, the Greaves–Wallace theorem and the global theorem are corollaries. Conditionalization on content is optimal exactly when the weighting is content-sufficient. That condition holds for agents certain that their evidence is partitional, for weightings symmetric under relabelling worlds, and for some cases with no partitional situation in view. It fails for almost every weighting and for every agent certain of a non-partitional situation. Nor is conditionalization the worst-case optimal rule.

What follows is a relocation of the debate. The global argument does not show that externalist complications wash out once rules are evaluated across situations. It shows that they are averaged away by a weighting that makes the fact of learning uninformative. The question to ask of a defence of conditionalization is therefore not whether agents can respond to more than the content of their evidence, but whether their credences about how they learn are content-sufficient, and, if they are not, what standard other than the agent's own expectation could recommend conditionalization to her. I have not shown that no such standard exists. I have shown that the global argument, read as an expected-accuracy argument, does not supply one.
