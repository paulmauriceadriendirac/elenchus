# Ignorable Learning: What the Total-Accuracy Argument for Conditionalization Rests On

**Abstract.** Meehan and Zhang argue that conditionalization is the rational response even to opaque evidence. Among rules that give the same posterior whenever the same proposition is learned, it has the highest total expected accuracy across updating problems. They show that this holds exactly when the weighting of problems is extensional, and they leave open how extensionality relates to evidence internalism and whether it is explanatory bedrock. I show that every weighting of updating problems is a probabilistic model of how evidence arrives. Extensionality is the statisticians' condition of coarsening at random imposed on that model, which makes it the distributional form of transparency. It does not require that only transparent problems be counted. But a standard that counts opaque problems satisfies it only on a knife-edge: reweighting any one opaque problem breaks it, and what holds the edge is the symmetry of the standard. The argument therefore rests on a neutrality requirement on how rules are evaluated, and importance-weighting of propositions respects it exactly when learning nothing new leaves credences unchanged. The general moral is that what threatens conditionalization is not opaque evidence but non-ignorable learning.

## 1. Introduction

Accuracy arguments for conditionalization compare updating plans by their expected accuracy. Greaves and Wallace (2006) showed that when learning is transparent, so that whenever one learns $p$ one also learns that $p$ is what one learned, conditionalization has the highest expected accuracy among plans that respond only to what is learned. Without transparency the result fails: Schoenfield (2017) showed that the best such plan in general conditions on the proposition that one has learned $p$. Externalists about evidence hold that learning is sometimes opaque, so they must give up either the accuracy argument or conditionalization, unless the argument can be rebuilt (for one rebuilding, see Zendejas Medina 2024).

Meehan and Zhang (2025) offer such a rebuilding. They impose a normative constraint, *global evidential constancy*: same prior, same evidence, same posterior, whatever learning situation one is in (314–315). They then evaluate rules by their total expected accuracy, summed over all factive updating problems that share a prior space (329). Conditionalization uniquely maximizes this total among globally evidentially constant rules (Fact 10, 330). They ask why every problem should count equally, and answer with a characterization: conditionalization is optimal for a weighting of problems if and only if the weighting is *extensional* (Fact 11, 337). They end uncertain about this condition. They say they are "getting close to the bedrock of normative explanations" (337). They see "a strong connection between extensionality on the one hand, and evidence internalism on the other" (338), while holding that "one does not have to be an internalist to subscribe to extensionality" (338). And they conclude that "the important question is not whether evidence internalism is true, but whether the (total) expected accuracy of an updating rule should be evaluated in a way that satisfies extensionality" (339).

This essay sharpens that question by saying what extensionality is. My thesis:

> Extensionality is coarsening at random imposed on the model of learning implicit in the evaluator's weighting: the condition that receiving a proposition carries no information about the state beyond that proposition's truth. It is weaker than counting only transparent problems. But when opaque problems are counted it holds only on a knife-edge, held in place by the symmetry of the standard. The total-accuracy argument therefore rests on a neutrality requirement on how rules are evaluated, and an externalist can accept it only by holding that the evaluation of rules should abstract from the systematic ways in which evidence fails to be transparent.

The argument runs as follows. Each state contributes the same total weight however the weights are set, so every weighting determines a *coarsening mechanism*: a probability, at each state, of receiving each true proposition (§3.1). Read this way, Meehan and Zhang's Lemma 1 is the Greaves–Wallace argument for an agent who learns which report she received, and their Lemma 2 and Fact 12 are instances of known facts about coarsening at random (§3.2). Their own uniform total is a model of random yes/no questions, but extensional standards need not count transparent problems at all (§3.3). They must, however, be exactly balanced (§4), and the natural sources of balance are symmetry and propriety. Importance that attaches to propositions keeps the balance exactly when the favoured rule obeys *No News*: on learning nothing new, keep the prior (§5). The general moral (§6) is that the line between conditionalization and its externalist rivals runs between ignorable and non-ignorable learning, not between transparent and opaque evidence.

The statistical facts I use are not mine. Heitjan and Rubin (1991) formulated coarsening at random, a generalization of missing at random, as a condition for ignoring the coarsening mechanism in inference. Grünwald and Halpern (2003) used it to explain when conditioning in a space that ignores the protocol by which information arrives gives the right answer. Gill and Grünwald (2008) characterized the set of such mechanisms geometrically. Meehan and Zhang do not draw on this literature. What is new here is the identification of their weightings with coarsening mechanisms and what it shows about their argument: the reduction of their uniform total to yes/no questions, the knife-edge result, the neutrality and propriety criteria for extensionality, the equivalence of extensionality with No News for separable importance, and the failure of maximin and minimax-regret alternatives.

For readers outside formal epistemology the point is this. To defend a rule "in general" one must say how much each situation counts. Any such choice behaves exactly like a hypothesis about how one comes to learn things. The defence of conditionalization works exactly when that hypothesis makes the manner of learning uninformative. That condition is fragile when the situations counted include the externalist's hard cases, and what sustains it is a demand that the standard play no favourites among states.

## 2. The total-accuracy argument

I follow Meehan and Zhang's framework. A *prior space* $(S,\pi)$ is a finite set of states, $|S| = n \ge 2$, with a regular probability $\pi$ (290). A *learning situation* $E$ maps each state $s$ to the strongest proposition learned at $s$, a set containing $s$. So evidence is factive, as they assume (290 n. 4). $(S,\pi,E)$ is an *updating problem*, and an *updating rule* returns for each problem and state a posterior over $S$ (291). The problem is *transparent* if $E(s) \subseteq [E = E(s)]$ for every $s$, where $[E=E(s)]$ is the set of states at which $E(s)$ is learned; otherwise it is *opaque* (292–294). For factive $E$, transparency is partitionality (294 n. 5). Accuracy is measured by a strictly proper score $A$, higher being better (296). BCOND recommends $\pi(\cdot\mid E(s))$; META-COND recommends $\pi(\cdot\mid E=E(s))$; the dogmatic rule recommends $\pi$ (291–292). META-COND has the highest expected accuracy among evidentially constant rules in every problem (Fact 2, 300, from Schoenfield 2017). In *Dana*, a problem about the weight of butter with prior $(0.2, 0.6, 0.2)$ over 15, 16 and 17 oz., one learns $\{15,16\}$ at 15, everything at 16 and $\{16,17\}$ at 17. There the dogmatic rule beats BCOND under the Brier score (Fact 3, 302).

Global evidential constancy conjoins evidential constancy with *noncontrastiveness*: two problems on the same prior space that deliver the same proposition at a state get the same posterior there (309, 314–315). Meehan and Zhang defend it as a normative constraint, not a followability constraint (314, 317). A rule satisfies it iff it is given by a map from a prior space and a proposition to a posterior (Fact 5, 316). I write $f(p)$ for its output on $p$. BCOND and the dogmatic rule qualify, and META-COND does not (316). Every such rule is beaten in some problem by another (Fact 8, 325), so they evaluate rules "in general". The *total expected accuracy* of $f$ sums its expected accuracy over the set $\mathcal{E}$ of all factive learning situations (329). *Hi-TEA* says that a rule is rational iff, among globally evidentially constant rules, it has the highest total relative to every prior space (330). BCOND does, uniquely (Fact 10, 330, 333).

In §5.3 a *weighting* $\lambda$ assigns each $E\in\mathcal E$ a non-negative weight, positive somewhere, and the weighted total is $\mathrm{TEA}_\lambda(f) = \sum_{E}\lambda(E)\sum_s\pi(s)A(f_{(S,\pi,E)}(s),s)$ (335). Each proposition $p$ induces $\lambda_p(s) = \sum_{E:\,E(s)=p}\lambda(E)$, and $\lambda$ is *extensional* if $\lambda_p(s)$ depends only on whether $p$ is true at $s$ (336). By factivity, this says that $\lambda_p$ is constant on $p$. For globally evidentially constant $f$,
$$\mathrm{TEA}_\lambda(f) = \sum_s \pi(s) \sum_{p \ni s} \lambda_p(s)\, A(f(p), s) \qquad (\ast)$$
(345). Lemma 1 (344): the optimal such rule recommends $\pi(\cdot\mid\lambda_p)$, where $\pi(s\mid\lambda_p) \propto \pi(s)\lambda_p(s)$. Lemma 2 (345): $\pi(\cdot\mid\lambda_p) = \pi(\cdot\mid p)$ for all $\pi$ and $p$ iff $\lambda$ is extensional. Hence Fact 11. Fact 12 (338): a point mass on one problem is extensional iff the problem is transparent.

## 3. Weightings are models of learning

### 3.1 The budget identity

**Observation.** For every weighting and every state $s$, $\sum_{p\ni s}\lambda_p(s) = \Lambda$, the total weight. Each learning situation delivers exactly one proposition at $s$.

So $q_\lambda(p\mid s) = \lambda_p(s)/\Lambda$ is a probability distribution over the propositions true at $s$: a *coarsening mechanism* in the statisticians' sense, which replaces each state with a set containing it (Gill and Grünwald 2008, §2). I call $(\lambda_p(s))$ the *profile* of $\lambda$.

**Proposition 1.** (a) Every coarsening mechanism is $q_\lambda$ for some weighting $\lambda$. (b) $\mathrm{TEA}_\lambda(f)/\Lambda$ is the expected accuracy of responding to each received proposition $p$ with $f(p)$, for an agent with prior $\pi$ who receives $p$ at $s$ with probability $q_\lambda(p\mid s)$. (c) The rule of Lemma 1 conditions on the event that $p$ was received.

*Proof.* (a) Put $\lambda(E) = \prod_s q(E(s)\mid s)$, the distribution of a learning situation that draws a proposition independently at each state. Summing over the other states' draws gives $\lambda_p(s) = q(p\mid s)$. (b) Divide $(\ast)$ by $\Lambda$. (c) $\Pr(s \mid p \text{ received}) \propto \pi(s)q_\lambda(p\mid s) \propto \pi(s)\lambda_p(s)$. ∎

So Lemma 1 is, in effect, the Greaves–Wallace argument run in an enlarged problem. Its states are pairs of a state and a report, and in it the agent learns transparently which report she received. Hi-TEA under $\lambda$ selects the rule that META-COND would recommend to an agent whose opinions about how she learns are given by $q_\lambda$. The rule is globally evidentially constant only because those opinions are fixed in the standard instead of being consulted by the rule.

Two notions of equivalence must be kept apart. Weightings are *ranking-equivalent* if their profiles are proportional, so that they induce the same mechanism. By $(\ast)$ they then rank all globally evidentially constant rules alike. They are *selection-equivalent* if they select the same optimal rules. By Lemma 1 this requires only that, for each $p$, $\lambda_p$ and $\mu_p$ be proportional on $p$, with a factor that may vary with $p$. Hi-TEA uses only selection.

### 3.2 Extensionality is coarsening at random

A mechanism is *coarsening at random* (CAR) if, for each $p$, the probability of receiving $p$ is the same at every state in $p$ (Gill and Grünwald 2008, eq. 2.1). By the Observation:

> $\lambda$ is extensional if and only if $q_\lambda$ is CAR.

Meehan and Zhang's results then fall into place. Lemma 2 says that conditioning on the receipt of $p$ agrees with conditioning on $p$, for every prior, exactly under CAR. This is the finite, Bayesian core of two classical points. Heitjan and Rubin (1991) give conditions, CAR among them, under which the coarsening mechanism "can be ignored when drawing Bayesian and likelihood inferences". And Grünwald and Halpern (2003, abstract) present CAR as the criterion that "characterizes when 'naive' conditioning in a naive space works", a naive space being one that ignores the protocol by which information arrives. Fact 12 says that a deterministic mechanism is CAR iff it is a partition. The closure of extensional weightings under mixture (336) is the convexity of the set of CAR mechanisms, which Gill and Grünwald show to be a polytope.

Extensionality is therefore *transparency in distribution*. In a transparent problem, learning $p$ and learning that one has learned $p$ come to the same thing. Under a CAR mechanism they need not coincide, but they have the same evidential import: receiving $p$ is evidence of nothing beyond $p$.

### 3.3 Three questions about internalism

Whether extensionality is "disguised internalism" now splits into three questions.

*Does every extensional standard select what an internalist standard selects?* Yes, trivially. If every $\lambda_p$ is non-zero, an extensional $\lambda$ selects BCOND, as does the uniform weighting on transparent yes/no problems. Meehan and Zhang's own standard is internalist in a stronger sense. For $\emptyset\ne q\ne S$, the *yes/no problem* $E_q$ delivers $q$ on $q$ and $S\setminus q$ off it, and $E_S$ delivers $S$ everywhere. These are the $2^{n-1}$ problems in $\mathcal Y$.

**Proposition 2.** For every globally evidentially constant $f$, the total over $\mathcal{E}$ equals $2^{(n-1)^2}$ times the total over $\mathcal{Y}$.

*Proof.* The uniform profile on $\mathcal E$ is $(2^{n-1})^{n-1}$ on $p$, Meehan and Zhang's $\alpha_S$ (344). The uniform profile on $\mathcal Y$ is $1$ on $p$, since only $E_p$ delivers $p$ at a $p$-state. Apply $(\ast)$. ∎

The uniform mechanism is the random-colouring model: colour each state red or blue with a fair coin and learn the colour of the actual state. Fact 10 then follows from Greaves and Wallace's theorem (Fact 1, 298) summand by summand, since BCOND is best in each transparent yes/no problem. The result lies close to the surface of Meehan and Zhang's proof of Fact 10 (332) and of their use of yes/no problems for Fact 7 (324–325). Stating it makes plain that their headline standard cannot tell the externalist's world of opaque learning from a world of random yes/no questions.

*Must an extensional standard count only transparent problems, or be ranking-equivalent to one that does?* No. Let $\lambda^\circ$ be the uniform weighting on the opaque problems in $\mathcal E$.

**Proposition 3.** $\lambda^\circ_p(s) = 2^{(n-1)^2} - B_{n-|p|}$ on $p$, where $B_m$ is the $m$-th Bell number, so $\lambda^\circ$ is extensional and selects BCOND. For $n\ge3$, no weighting supported on transparent problems ranks globally evidentially constant rules as $\lambda^\circ$ does.

*Proof.* A transparent problem delivering $p$ at $s$ is a partition with $p$ as a cell, and there are $B_{n-|p|}$ of them. Subtract from $\alpha_S$. For the second claim, let $\mu$ be supported on partitions. Any partition with cell $S\setminus\{s\}$ also has cell $\{s\}$, so $\mu_{\{s\}}(s)\ge\mu_{S\setminus\{s\}}$. But $\lambda^\circ_{\{s\}}(s) < \lambda^\circ_{S\setminus\{s\}}$ because $B_{n-1}\ge 2$. Take rules $f,g$ that differ only on $\{s\}$ and $S\setminus\{s\}$. Suppose $f$ loses $\delta_1$ in expected accuracy on $\{s\}$ and gains $\delta_2$ on $S\setminus\{s\}$, with $1\le\delta_1/\delta_2<\lambda^\circ_{S\setminus\{s\}}/\lambda^\circ_{\{s\}}$. Such rules exist by continuity of $A$. Then every such $\mu$ ranks $g$ at least as high as $f$, and $\lambda^\circ$ ranks $f$ strictly higher. ∎

The simplest case is a standard example in the CAR literature (Gill and Grünwald 2008, Example 1, which they credit to earlier work of Gill, van der Laan and Robins).

> *Cyclic Informants.* There are three states. Informant A reports "1 or 2" at 1, "2 or 3" at 2 and "3 or 1" at 3. Informant B reports "1 or 3" at 1, "1 or 2" at 2 and "2 or 3" at 3. Each has weight 1.

Both problems are opaque, yet each report has weight 1 at both of its states, so the weighting is CAR. Its support contains no partition, so it is not a mixture of transparent problems.

So Meehan and Zhang are right that extensionality does not require internalism about the problems counted, and right that the two are strongly connected. The connection is this: extensionality requires that the standard's model of learning share the evidential signature of transparency. The third question, which I take up next, is whether that requirement is stable.

## 4. A knife-edge, and what holds it

**Proposition 4.** Let $\Sigma$ be a set of learning situations. Every weighting supported on $\Sigma$ is extensional iff every member of $\Sigma$ is transparent. Moreover, if $\lambda$ is extensional and $E$ is opaque, then adding weight $\varepsilon>0$ to $E$ yields a non-extensional weighting.

*Proof.* Profiles are linear in the weighting. If $\lambda$ and $\lambda+\varepsilon\delta_E$ were both extensional, so would be their difference, $\varepsilon$ times the point mass on $E$, contradicting Fact 12. The "if" direction is Meehan and Zhang's observation (336). For "only if", the point mass on any opaque member of $\Sigma$ is supported on $\Sigma$. ∎

Standards that count only transparent problems vindicate BCOND however the problems are weighted. Standards that count opaque problems vindicate it only when the opaque problems' biases cancel exactly. Add any weight to *Dana* within Meehan and Zhang's uniform total and the optimal response to $\{15,16\}$ is no longer $\pi(\cdot\mid\{15,16\})$. The CAR literature notes the same fragility from the side of mechanisms. Of *Cyclic Informants*, implemented by tossing a coin to choose the informant, Gill and Grünwald write: "If the coin is biased the CAR property can be lost" (2008, Example 2). Grünwald and Halpern (2003, abstract) find that CAR "holds rather infrequently".

What holds the edge in Meehan and Zhang's case is symmetry. A permutation $\sigma$ of $S$ acts on learning situations by $(\sigma E)(\sigma s) = \sigma[E(s)]$. Call $\lambda$ *neutral* if $\lambda(\sigma E)=\lambda(E)$ for all $\sigma$ and $E$.

**Proposition 5.** Every neutral weighting is extensional.

*Proof.* Neutrality gives $\lambda_{\sigma[p]}(\sigma s) = \lambda_p(s)$. For $s,t\in p$ the transposition of $s$ and $t$ maps $p$ to itself. ∎

The uniform total, $\lambda^\circ$ and *Cyclic Informants* are all neutral, and averaging any weighting over relabellings of the states makes it neutral. Neutrality is sufficient but not necessary: a point mass on a single partition is extensional but not neutral. It nevertheless supplies a criterion that does not presuppose conditionalization. A standard that plays no favourites among states, treating a state only as the bearer of the propositions true at it, vindicates BCOND.

A second criterion addresses the reading Meehan and Zhang give the weights, "the epistemic importance of updating accurately in response to learning proposition p at state s" (336). By $(\ast)$, a weighting scores each response $f(p)$ by the induced rule $A^\lambda_p(c,s) = \lambda_p(s)A(c,s)$ on $p$.

**Proposition 6.** $A^\lambda_p$ is strictly proper on $p$ iff $\lambda_p$ is a positive constant on $p$. Hence a weighting with every $\lambda_p$ non-zero is extensional iff every induced score is strictly proper.

*Proof.* For $b$ a probability on $p$, $\sum_s b(s)\lambda_p(s)A(c,s)$ is proportional to the expectation of $A(c,\cdot)$ under $b$ reweighted by $\lambda_p$. By strict propriety of $A$, its unique maximizer is that reweighting, which is $b$ for every $b$ iff $\lambda_p$ is constant. ∎

On the importance reading, then, a non-extensional standard evaluates responses to some proposition by an improper measure. Such a measure leads an agent with any credences over the $p$-states to expect other credences to score better. Accuracy-first epistemologists reject improper measures. Meehan and Zhang assume strict propriety for $A$ (296), so anyone who accepts their reading and their scoring constraint must accept extensionality. Neither criterion mentions conditionalization. Neutrality constrains the weights' symmetry, and propriety constrains the induced scores.

Neutrality has a cost, and the cost locates the dispute. States are rarely bare labels. In *Dana* they are weights on a scale, and the opacity of her problem comes from that structure: one learns less near the middle of the range than at its ends. A neutral standard gives the problem in which 16 oz. is the uninformative state the same weight as the one in which 15 oz. is, so it treats the scale as if it had no shape. That is what makes neutral standards CAR. It is also what an externalist who locates opacity in margins for error has reason to doubt, if the standard is meant to reflect the situations agents are in. Propriety does not settle the matter either. It binds the importance reading of the weights. On the likelihood reading, a non-CAR profile is not an improper score but a hypothesis about how one learns, and the selected rule is the one appropriate to that hypothesis.

## 5. Importance that attaches to propositions

The objection Meehan and Zhang take most seriously is that problems differ in importance. "[P]erhaps the updating problem where one learns the truth of some laws of nature is more important than one where one learns the number of stones in Wisconsin" (335). Importance of this kind breaks neutrality. Does it break extensionality?

Suppose importance attaches to propositions, in the sense that the profile's dependence on the state is common to all propositions. Then the profile is separable: $\lambda_p(s) = a(p)g(s)$ or $\lambda_p(s) = a(p)+g(s)$ on $p$, with $a(S)>0$ and $g>0$ in the first case. Let $A(s) = \sum_{p\ni s}a(p)$. Say that a rule satisfies *No News* if, on learning $S$, it recommends the prior.

**Proposition 7.** (a) For any weighting with $\lambda_S$ not identically zero, the optimal rule satisfies No News iff $\lambda_S$ is constant. (b) For separable profiles, the budget identity fixes $g$: $g = \Lambda/A$ in the multiplicative case and $g = (\Lambda - A)/2^{n-1}$ in the additive case. Consequently extensionality, No News and constancy of $A$ are equivalent.

*Proof.* (a) Lemma 1 and regularity. (b) Sum the profile over $p\ni s$ and use the Observation. Extensionality requires $\lambda_S$ to be constant, so $g$ is constant, so $A$ is constant. Conversely, constant $A$ makes $g$, and hence every $\lambda_p$ on $p$, constant. ∎

Three natural aggregations of an importance function $h$ on propositions have separable profiles: weighting each problem by the product of the importances of what it delivers at each state, by their sum, or by the sum over the distinct propositions it delivers. I checked the last by exact enumeration for $n = 3, 4$. The equivalences also hold when only opaque problems are counted, since the transparent part is extensional and gives learning $S$ the same weight everywhere. For the product form, $\lambda_p(s) = \Lambda h(p)/H(s)$ with $H(s) = \sum_{p\ni s}h(p)$, and the optimal rule conditionalizes the *tilted prior* $\pi(s)/H(s)$ on what is learned.

So within this class No News is the probe that detects every departure, because $S$ is true at every state and its weight registers any state-dependence common to all propositions. Proposition 7 also explains why importance produces a learning tilt at all. Weights live on learning situations, and each situation delivers exactly one proposition per state, so each state has a fixed budget. Where more important truths compete for that budget, each proposition's share shrinks.

> *Enough Butter.* Take Dana's prior. Suppose it matters especially to learn that there are at least 16 oz., enough for a recipe: $h(\{16,17\}) = 5$, and $h = 1$ otherwise.

Then $H = (4,8,8)$ and the tilted prior is $(1/3, 1/2, 1/6)$. On learning $\{15,16\}$, the importance-weighted standard favours $(2/5, 3/5, 0)$ over BCOND's $(1/4,3/4,0)$. On learning nothing new it moves from $(0.2,0.6,0.2)$ to $(1/3,1/2,1/6)$. Being globally evidentially constant, the rule must do so in Noah's problem too, where nothing is ever learned (312). There Noah expects it to score $-53/45 \approx -1.18$ under the Brier score, against $-1.12$ for keeping his prior.[^1] In the weighted population of problems, learning nothing is relatively more frequent at 15, where nothing important is there to be learned, so the standard treats learning nothing as evidence for 15.

Balance is easy to state. If $h(p) = h(S\setminus p)$ for every proper $p$, then $H$ is constant, because each state lies in exactly one of $p$ and $S\setminus p$. Importance that attaches to *questions* is harmless, while importance that favours one answer to a question over the other is not. The laws-versus-stones example reads naturally as a claim about questions.

Meehan and Zhang's non-Bayesian goes further: "it also matters how likely it is for one to learn p at s, and perhaps also how epistemically important p is as a proposition at s" (337–338). Importance relative to a state is not separable, and Proposition 7 does not touch it. Meehan and Zhang tie this option to "considerations of contingent epistemic importance (Levinstein 2019)" (339). Two consequences follow from §§3–4. First, the non-Bayesian's two considerations are a single parameter. By the Observation, every importance profile is a model of how likely one is to learn $p$ at $s$, and the rule it selects is the one appropriate to that model. Second, on the importance reading, Proposition 6 gives the price. State-relative importance scores responses to $p$ improperly, and the selected rule shifts credence toward the $p$-states where accuracy about $p$ matters more.

## 6. The moral: ignorability, not transparency

The CAR reading of Lemma 2 applies to agents as well as evaluators. Consider an agent with prior $\pi$ over $S$ whose opinions about how she learns form a mechanism $q$. Conditioning on her having received $p$ coincides with conditioning on $p$, for her credences over $S$ and for every prior, iff $q$ is CAR. An agent who thinks *Cyclic Informants* describes her situation, with a fair coin choosing the informant, never learns that she has learned what she has. Yet for every proposition she might receive, the posterior over $S$ that Schoenfield's reasoning recommends is the conditional one. If the coin is biased, the two come apart. The divergence between BCOND and META-COND that drives the externalist challenge comes from learning that is non-ignorable by the agent's lights. Opacity alone does not produce it.[^2]

The total-accuracy argument relocates the same condition. It vindicates BCOND exactly when the evaluator's mechanism is CAR. By Proposition 4 that holds robustly only if the evaluator counts transparent problems alone, and otherwise only through exact balance, which neutrality supplies. This tells us who can accept the argument. Internalists can. Externalists can if they hold that the standard for evaluating rules should be neutral between states, abstracting from the structure that makes their evidence opaque, such as margins for error or the asymmetry between cases where $p$ is learned and $p$-cases where it is not. An externalist who holds that the standard should reflect how agents actually or typically learn cannot, if she thinks opacity is systematic. Whenever being in some $p$-states makes one more likely to learn $p$ than being in others, the population of problems is non-CAR, and the rule it selects is not BCOND. Meehan and Zhang's suggestion that Bayesianism is a theory of "high-level" rationality, coexisting with a "low-level" theory that tracks individual dispositions and environments (338), now has exact content. The high-level standard is a CAR standard, and Proposition 4 says that it remains one only by abstracting from the low-level structure, not by approximating it.

The comparison with neighbouring proposals is now exact. Meehan and Zhang report that Carr (2021) uses a formally similar aggregate but reads it as "the agent's subjective estimate of how accurate an updating rule is, given her uncertainty about what she might learn at a given state" (330 n. 35). On that reading the argument vindicates BCOND for an agent iff her uncertainty is CAR, which is just the agent-level fact above. Meehan and Zhang's objective reading, a measure "for any agent, irrespective of their uncertainties about their evidential situations" (330 n. 35), needs a reason why the objective standard should be CAR, and neutrality is the natural candidate. They also report that Zendejas Medina (2024) shows that "for each proposition p, conditionalizing on p is the best conditional updating plan for p" (287 n. 2). By Lemma 1, $\mathrm{TEA}_\lambda(f) = \sum_p \mathbb{E}_\pi[\lambda_p]\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi(\cdot\mid\lambda_p)}[A(f(p),\cdot)]$. This is a positive combination of expected accuracies conditional on the propositions learned exactly when $\lambda$ is extensional. Under CAR, then, the total-accuracy and conditional-accuracy approaches coincide, and outside it they come apart.

The wider debate has framed the threat to conditionalization as a threat from opaque evidence. The analysis here suggests a different framing. Conditionalization is vindicated by expected accuracy, for an agent or for an evaluator, exactly when the relevant model of how evidence arrives is ignorable. Transparency is the robust special case. Accuracy-first defences of conditionalization for externalists must therefore either argue that the relevant learning model is ignorable or argue that evaluation should be neutral. They cannot simply count opaque cases in.

## 7. Objections and replies

**Objection 1: every weighting is a learning hypothesis.** The uniform standard is the hypothesis of random yes/no questions (Proposition 2). So privileging CAR standards is just preferring one hypothesis to another.

*Reply.* The first sentence is granted; it is the thesis of §3. But the criteria in §4 are not hypotheses about how agents learn. Neutrality is a symmetry of the standard, and propriety is a constraint the accuracy framework already imposes on scores. What the non-Bayesian must reject is one of these, not the Bayesian's empirical picture of learning. That is the precise form of Meehan and Zhang's stand-off, and I do not claim to have settled it.

**Objection 2: the knife-edge is harmless.** The optimal rule $\pi(\cdot\mid\lambda_p)$ varies continuously with $\lambda$, so under small perturbations of the uniform total the optimal rule stays close to BCOND. With weight $1/10$ added to *Dana*, the optimal response to $\{15,16\}$ is $(161/641, 480/641, 0)$, against $(1/4, 3/4, 0)$.

*Reply.* Granted, as far as it goes. Small perturbations still answer the question Meehan and Zhang start from: why not the truth rule? But Hi-TEA claims that BCOND is uniquely rational, and that claim survives only at the edge. Departures that reflect where agents actually are need not be small, because their size depends on the share of weight the favoured problems carry. If *Dana* is given as much weight as the other 63 problems together, the optimal response to $\{15,16\}$ becomes $(5/8, 3/8, 0)$, and on learning nothing the rule moves from $(0.2,0.6,0.2)$ to $(1/17, 15/17, 1/17)$. The approximate vindication also needs a reason why the standard should lie near a CAR one, and that reason is again neutrality.

**Objection 3: why sum at all?** A cautious evaluator might rank rules by their worst case, and then no weights are needed.

*Reply.* Neither of the standard alternatives favours BCOND. A factive learning situation chooses the proposition learned at each state independently, so the worst expected accuracy of $f$ is $\sum_s\pi(s)\min_{p\ni s}A(f(p),s)$. That is at most $\sum_s\pi(s)A(f(S),s)$, which by propriety is at most the dogmatic rule's value. With Dana's prior and the Brier score, BCOND's worst case is $-1.188$, in Dana's own problem (332), against $-1.12$. Minimax regret fares no better. In any problem the best globally evidentially constant rule matches META-COND's expected accuracy, by mimicking it there (327). BCOND's maximal regret is $1.188$, in Dana's problem, where META-COND is the truth rule. The dogmatic rule's is $1.12$, attained where everything is learned. Dominance does not single out BCOND either. It is undominated (Fact 7, 324), but so is the one-shot rule tailored to Dana (326 n. 32). A vindication along these lines must aggregate by weights, and so faces the question of §§3–5.

## 8. Conclusion

Meehan and Zhang showed that their argument for conditionalization stands or falls with extensionality. I have argued that extensionality is coarsening at random for the model of learning that every weighting implicitly is. It is the distributional form of transparency. It does not require counting only transparent problems, but with opaque problems counted it holds only on a knife-edge. Symmetry of the standard keeps it there, propriety of the induced scores requires it, and proposition-attached importance preserves it exactly when No News holds. Their own uniform standard is a model of random yes/no questions. I have not shown that neutrality is mandatory. A non-Bayesian may hold that the evaluation of rules should register how agents actually learn, and then the argument does not reach her. What I have shown is that this, and not internalism, is where the dispute lies. My results are for finite spaces and factive evidence; for infinite non-partitional settings, see Easwaran and Nielsen (2025).
