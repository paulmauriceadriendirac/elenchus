# Flattening Fails for Counterfactuals: Cumulative Supposition and Nested Conditionals

**Abstract.** Flattening says that "if p, then if p and q, r" is equivalent to "if p and q, r". It is the fragment of Import-Export in which the inner antecedent entails the outer one, and Dorr and Mandelkern have shown that adding it to Stalnaker's logic C2 yields the logic of a general form of sequence semantics, while leaving open what intuitive semantics could validate it. I argue that Flattening is false for counterfactuals. In C2 with Flattening, the mere absence of counterfactual connection between two unrealized possibilities entails that, had either been realized, it would have depended counterfactually on the other's non-realization. A simple case makes the premises true and the conclusion false, and a variant refutes either half of Flattening in any normal conditional logic. I then prove that Flattening holds on a selection frame exactly when imaging on that frame is cumulative, and explain why indicative supposition is cumulative while counterfactual supposition is not.

## 1. Introduction

Suppose something, and then, within that supposition, suppose something stronger. Do you end where you would have ended had you supposed the stronger thing from the start? For supposition by conditioning the answer is trivially yes: conditioning on $Y$ and then on some $X$ that entails $Y$ gives the same result as conditioning on $X$. For a conditional connective $>$, the question becomes whether the following principle holds:

$$\text{(Flattening)}\qquad p > ((p \wedge q) > r) \;\equiv\; (p \wedge q) > r.$$

Flattening is the instance of Import-Export, $p > (q > r) \equiv (p \wedge q) > r$, in which the inner antecedent entails the outer one: put $p \wedge q$ for $q$ and simplify $p \wedge p \wedge q$. Import-Export itself is widely rejected or restricted. It sits badly with Identity (Mandelkern 2021) and with classical conjunction (Mandelkern 2020), and it is the common source of a family of triviality results (Khoo and Mandelkern 2019). Flattening is much weaker, and it has recently been given a precise logical home. Dorr and Mandelkern (2026) show that the logic of sequence semantics, the semantics van Fraassen (1976) developed in connection with Stalnaker's Thesis, is C2.FS when sequences have length $\omega$ and C2.F when sequences of any ordinal length are allowed. Here C2 is Stalnaker's (1968) logic, C2.F adds Flattening, and C2.FS adds a further principle, Sequentiality. Dorr and Mandelkern observe that a similarity-based semantics cannot validate Flattening, and they leave it as an open problem for proponents of C2.F to supply an intuitive semantics that does (2026, §4.1). They also note that it remains open whether a restricted form of Stalnaker's Thesis could yield a restricted form of Flattening (§7).

I defend the following thesis. *Flattening is false for counterfactual conditionals. What it encodes is the cumulativity of supposition, a property that supposition by conditioning has and counterfactual supposition lacks; so Flattening is at most a principle for indicatives.* The thesis does not say that Flattening is valid for indicatives, nor that sequence semantics is mistaken about them. On those questions I argue only that my case against Flattening does not transfer, and that the probabilistic commitment Flattening carries for indicatives is benign.

The increment over existing work has three parts. First, Dorr and Mandelkern's observation concerns a semantics: similarity orderings do not validate Flattening. A proponent of C2.F can take that as a point against similarity. I give an argument that depends on no semantics (§3). In C2.F, two purely negative premises, which hold whenever two unrealized possibilities are counterfactually unconnected, entail a positive claim of counterfactual dependence. A simple case makes the premises true and the conclusion false. A variant shows that either half of Flattening fails in any normal conditional logic, with or without Conditional Excluded Middle. Second, I prove that Flattening is valid on a selection frame if and only if imaging on that frame is cumulative (§4). This turns the request for an intuitive semantics into a precise requirement, and it shows why no account of counterfactual supposition can meet it. Third, I show that, given Stalnaker's Thesis, the probabilistic content of Flattening is a restricted form of Lewis's Factorization principle which, unlike Factorization, is satisfiable without triviality (§5). That identifies what a probabilistic route to Flattening must establish, though I do not claim to complete the route.

The point matters beyond the logic of conditionals. Flattening is the most cautious step one can take beyond Stalnaker's logic toward Import-Export. The results show that even this step separates indicative from counterfactual conditionals, and that it does so along a clean line: whether a second, nested supposition is made from inside the scenario that the first supposition introduced. A unified theory of "if", a long-standing aim (Starr and Kocurek 2025, §7.3), must therefore let nesting work differently in the two cases. The argument also yields a counterexample to Import-Export for counterfactuals in its weakest fragment, with only Boolean components.

Section 2 sets out selection semantics and the frame condition for Flattening. Section 3 presents the case and the main argument. Section 4 proves the correspondence with imaging and draws the diagnosis. Section 5 explains why indicatives differ, Section 6 answers objections, and Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Flattening in selection semantics

The language has sentence letters, the Boolean connectives and a binary conditional $>$. A *selection model* is a triple $\langle W, f, V\rangle$. $W$ is a set of worlds and $V$ a valuation. The function $f$ takes a world $w$ and a set of worlds $X$ to a world $f(w,X)$, intuitively the $X$-world selected from the standpoint of $w$. Following Stalnaker (1968), $f$ satisfies three conditions. Success: $f(w,X) \in X$ when $X$ is non-empty; otherwise $f(w,X)$ is an absurd world at which every sentence is true. Centering: if $w \in X$ then $f(w,X) = w$. Uniformity: if $f(w,X) \in Y$ and $f(w,Y) \in X$ then $f(w,X) = f(w,Y)$. A conditional $A > C$ is true at $w$ iff $C$ is true at $f(w,[A])$, where $[A]$ is the set of worlds at which $A$ is true.

C2 is the logic of these models. It validates Conditional Excluded Middle (CEM), $(A > C) \vee (A > \neg C)$, as well as modus ponens and Identity, $A > A$. It is closed under two rules. RCEA: from $A \equiv A'$ infer $(A > C) \equiv (A' > C)$. RCK: from $(C_1 \wedge \dots \wedge C_n) \supset D$ infer $((A > C_1) \wedge \dots \wedge (A > C_n)) \supset (A > D)$. I call the logic given by these two rules alone CK, the minimal normal conditional logic. Every standard logic of counterfactuals contains it, including Lewis's (1973) VC, which rejects CEM.

**Observation 1.** Flattening is valid on a selection frame $\langle W, f\rangle$ iff
$$\text{(Flat)}\qquad f(f(w,Y),X) = f(w,X)\ \text{ whenever } X \subseteq Y.$$
The same holds for each direction of Flattening taken separately.

*Proof.* Let $P$ and $Q$ be the sets where $p$ and $q$ are true. The left side of Flattening is true at $w$ iff $r$ is true at $f(f(w,P), P \cap Q)$, and the right side iff $r$ is true at $f(w, P \cap Q)$. (Flat) makes these the same world. Conversely, suppose $f(f(w,Y),X) \neq f(w,X)$ for some non-empty $X \subseteq Y$. Let $p$ be true exactly on $Y$ and $q$ exactly on $X$. If $r$ is true only at $f(w,X)$, the right side is true at $w$ and the left side false. If $r$ is true only at $f(f(w,Y),X)$, the left side is true and the right side false. ∎

(Flat) says that the $X$-world selected from the $Y$-world selected from $w$ is the $X$-world selected from $w$. If selection picks the most similar world, (Flat) requires that, for $X \subseteq Y$, the $X$-world most similar to $w$'s nearest $Y$-world be $w$'s own nearest $X$-world. Nothing about similarity guarantees this. In sequence semantics, roughly, the points of evaluation are sequences of worlds, and a conditional with antecedent $X$ is evaluated at the tail of the sequence that begins with its first $X$-world. There (Flat) holds automatically: when $X \subseteq Y$, the first $X$-world in the tail that begins at the first $Y$-world is the first $X$-world in the whole sequence.

## 3. The Reunion

> *The Reunion.* Ann and Bob studied together and have not been in touch for years. Both missed last Saturday's college reunion. Ann has been working in Australia since January and never considered flying back for it. Bob lives near the venue and meant to come, but his babysitter cancelled that afternoon and he stayed home with his daughter. Nothing about either one's circumstances bears on the other's. Neither knew whether the other was coming, and nothing that would have brought one of them would have affected the other.

Write $A$ for "Ann came" and $B$ for "Bob came", and read $>$ counterfactually. The following are true:

(N1) $\neg(A > B)$: it is not the case that, had Ann come, Bob would have come.

(N2) $\neg(B > A)$: it is not the case that, had Bob come, Ann would have come.

The following is false:

(D) $(A \vee B) > [(A \wedge (B > \neg A)) \vee (B \wedge (A > \neg B))]$: had one of them come, whichever came would have stayed away had the other come.

Bob's coming needed only a babysitter who kept her word, while Ann's needed an intercontinental trip she never considered. So this is also true:

(P2) $(A \vee B) > (B \wedge \neg A)$: had one of them come, it would have been Bob.

Given (P2), (D) says that had one of them come, it would have been Bob, and he would have stayed home had Ann come. That is plainly false. In the scenario in which the babysitter keeps her word and Bob comes, Ann's flying in from Australia would have made no difference to his evening. The premises (N1) and (N2) are as weak as such judgments get. They do not assert that Bob would have stayed home had Ann come. They only deny that he would have come.

**Theorem 1.** In C2.F, (N1) and (N2) jointly entail (D).

*Proof.* Take a selection model satisfying (Flat), and suppose (N1) and (N2) are true at $w$. Then $[A]$ and $[B]$ are non-empty, since a conditional with an impossible antecedent is true. Moreover $f(w,[A]) \notin [B]$ and $f(w,[B]) \notin [A]$. Let $v = f(w, [A \vee B])$. Since $[A] \subseteq [A \vee B]$, (Flat) gives $f(v,[A]) = f(w,[A]) \notin [B]$. Likewise $f(v,[B]) = f(w,[B]) \notin [A]$. So $A > \neg B$ and $B > \neg A$ are both true at $v$. Since $v \in [A \vee B]$, either $v \in [A]$, and then $A \wedge (B > \neg A)$ is true at $v$, or $v \in [B]$, and then $B \wedge (A > \neg B)$ is. Either way (D) is true at $w$. ∎

Three remarks. First, the proof can also be run syntactically. CEM turns (N1) into $A > \neg B$. The right-to-left direction of Flattening, with $A \vee B$ for $p$, $B$ for $q$ and $\neg A$ for $r$, together with RCEA, yields $(A \vee B) > (B > \neg A)$. Similarly $(A \vee B) > (A > \neg B)$. Identity and RCK then assemble the consequent of (D). By Observation 1, the left-to-right direction would do the same work over selection frames. Second, (P2) plays no role in the proof; it only makes the falsity of (D) vivid. Indeed C2.F itself entails that, had one of them come, exactly one would have: if $v$ were in both $[A]$ and $[B]$, Centering would make $v$ its own selected $B$-world, contradicting $f(v,[B]) \notin [A]$. Third, I checked Theorem 1 by exhaustive constraint solving. Using the Z3 solver, I searched every selection function satisfying Success, Centering and Uniformity on frames of four and of five worlds. With (Flat) imposed, (N1), (N2) and the negation of (D) are jointly unsatisfiable; without it they are satisfiable. I also checked that a four-world model of the Reunion is a C2 model in which (N1), (N2), (P2) and the negation of (D) all hold. In that model worlds are fixed by whether Ann and whether Bob came, and selection minimizes a weighted count of differences in which Ann's coming costs more than Bob's.

Nothing depends on the details of the story. Take any two unrealized possibilities, neither of which would counterfactually have brought the other in its train. They satisfy (N1) and (N2), and C2.F then delivers, under the supposition that one of them was realized, a counterfactual dependence between them. Such pairs are everywhere: had I drunk tea this morning, or had a stranger in Lima bought an umbrella. If C2.F were the logic of counterfactuals, spurious counterfactual dependence would be everywhere too.

The theorem uses CEM, which C2.F contains and which Lewis (1973) rejects. A theorist who adds Flattening to a weaker logic fares no better.

**Proposition 2.** Let $L$ be any logic containing CK.
(a) If $L$ contains the left-to-right direction of Flattening, then $L \vdash ((A \vee B) > (A > B)) \supset (A > B)$.
(b) If $L$ contains the right-to-left direction, then $L \vdash (A > \neg B) \supset ((A \vee B) > (A > \neg B))$.

*Proof.* For (a), instantiate the left-to-right direction with $A \vee B$ for $p$, $A$ for $q$ and $B$ for $r$. This gives $((A \vee B) > (((A \vee B) \wedge A) > B)) \supset (((A \vee B) \wedge A) > B)$. Since $(A \vee B) \wedge A \equiv A$, RCEA replaces the inner antecedent, and RCK lets the replacement be made inside the consequent of the outer conditional. For (b), argue in the same way with $\neg B$ for $r$. ∎

In the Reunion, $(A \vee B) > (A > B)$ is true: had one of them come, it would have been Bob, and he would have come even if Ann had come too. But $A > B$ is false. And $A > \neg B$ is true, since had Ann come, Bob would still have stayed home, while $(A \vee B) > (A > \neg B)$ is false. So each direction of Flattening fails separately, in every normal conditional logic.

## 4. What Flattening says: cumulative supposition

Let $W$ be finite, $Pr$ a probability function on $W$, and $f$ a selection function. *Imaging* $Pr$ on a non-empty $X$ moves the probability of each world to the $X$-world selected from it:
$$Pr^X(u) = \sum_{w:\, f(w,X) = u} Pr(w).$$
Lewis (1976) introduced imaging as the operation under which the probability of a Stalnaker conditional equals the probability of its consequent. Since $X > \varphi$ is true at $w$ iff $\varphi$ is true at $f(w,X)$, we have $Pr(X > \varphi) = Pr^X(\varphi)$. Conditioning is the familiar model of supposing that something is in fact the case, and imaging is the familiar model of supposing that it had been the case. Eva, Shear and Fitelson (2022) relate the two operations to belief revision and belief update respectively.

Call a supposition operation $\ast$ *cumulative* if $(Pr \ast Y) \ast X = Pr \ast X$ whenever $X \subseteq Y$. Conditioning is cumulative: when $X \subseteq Y$, conditioning $Pr(\cdot \mid Y)$ on $X$ gives $Pr(\cdot \mid X \cap Y) = Pr(\cdot \mid X)$.

**Theorem 3.** For a selection frame $\langle W, f\rangle$ with $W$ finite, the following are equivalent:
(i) Flattening is valid on the frame;
(ii) the frame satisfies (Flat);
(iii) imaging on the frame is cumulative for every probability function on $W$.

*Proof.* (i) and (ii) are equivalent by Observation 1. Assume (ii). For $X \subseteq Y$,
$$(Pr^Y)^X(u) = \sum_{w:\, f(f(w,Y),X) = u} Pr(w) = \sum_{w:\, f(w,X) = u} Pr(w) = Pr^X(u).$$
Assume (iii), and let $Pr$ give probability 1 to a world $w$. Then $(Pr^Y)^X$ gives probability 1 to $f(f(w,Y),X)$ and $Pr^X$ gives it to $f(w,X)$, so these are the same world. ∎

Since $Pr(Y > (X > \varphi)) = (Pr^Y)^X(\varphi)$, condition (iii) says that the probability of a nested conditional whose inner antecedent entails its outer antecedent can always be computed by supposing the inner antecedent outright. Theorem 3 also gives Dorr and Mandelkern's request a precise content. An intuitive semantics that validates Flattening must be one on which successive suppositions are cumulative.

Now return to the Reunion, with all credence on the actual world. Imaging on $A \vee B$ moves all probability to the scenario in which Bob came. Imaging the result on $A$ moves it to a world in which both came. Imaging directly on $A$ moves it to a world in which Ann came and Bob did not. The resulting credence that Bob came is 1 in the first case and 0 in the second. The intuitive verdicts agree. Suppose that one of them had come, and then suppose further that Ann had come: we picture Bob there too. Suppose directly that Ann had come: we do not. This judgment concerns successive acts of supposition, not the truth values of embedded sentences, so it does not rest on intuitions about nested counterfactuals.

What goes wrong is general. A counterfactual supposition is made from a world, and what it adds to the scenario depends on what that world contains. A weak antecedent such as $A \vee B$ leaves open how it is realized, and the counterfactual scenario realizes it in a particular way. A stronger supposition made from inside that scenario keeps the realization when it can, because the realization is now part of the world from which the second supposition is made. Flattening requires that the realization always be discarded, so that the second supposition is in effect made from the original world. That is the right requirement for conditioning. Conditioning is made from an information state, not a world, and it never commits to a realization. Conditioning on $A \vee B$ removes the worlds where neither came and leaves the rest as they were, so further conditioning on $A$ finds the $A$-worlds exactly as it would have found them. It is the wrong requirement for counterfactual supposition.

Interventionist semantics confirms the diagnosis and marks where Flattening is safe. In structural-equation models (Galles and Pearl 1998; Halpern 2000; Briggs 2012), an antecedent that sets variables $S$ to values $s$ is evaluated by replacing the equations for those variables with constants. Intervening on $S$ and then on $S \cup T$ yields the same model as intervening on $S \cup T$ directly, since the second intervention resets the variables in $S$ to the values the first gave them. So counterfactuals whose antecedents are conjunctions of variable settings satisfy Flattening. Failures require an outer antecedent that can be realized in more than one way, such as a disjunction of settings. Those are the complex antecedents whose treatment within interventionism is still open (Starr and Kocurek 2025, §6.3). Suppose that, on a given treatment, at least one admissible way of realizing "had Ann or Bob come" sets Bob's variable. Then the inner antecedent "had Ann come" adds a setting of Ann's variable to a model in which Bob's is already fixed, and Bob stays. Interventionist theories reach the verdicts of Section 3 for the reason the diagnosis gives.

## 5. Why indicatives are different

The diagnosis predicts that Flattening's fate depends on whether supposition works by conditioning. On views on which if-clauses restrict a modal base (Kratzer 2012), restriction behaves like conditioning: restricting by $p$ and then by $p \wedge q$ is restricting by $p \wedge q$. The probabilistic version of the point is more informative.

Stalnaker's Thesis (Stalnaker 1970) says that $Pr(A > C) = Pr(C \mid A)$ whenever $Pr(A) > 0$. Consider a credence function that satisfies the Thesis for the simple conditional $(p \wedge q) > r$ and for the right-nested conditional $p > ((p \wedge q) > r)$, where the consequent of the latter is the simple conditional.

**Proposition 4.** Suppose $Pr(p \wedge q) > 0$, $Pr(p > ((p \wedge q) > r)) = Pr((p \wedge q) > r \mid p)$, and $Pr((p \wedge q) > r) = Pr(r \mid p \wedge q)$. Then the two sides of Flattening have the same probability iff
$$\text{(RS)}\qquad Pr((p \wedge q) > r \mid p) = Pr(r \mid p \wedge q).$$

*Proof.* Substitute the two assumed identities into the equation between the two sides. ∎

(RS) says that the Thesis for $(p \wedge q) > r$ survives learning $p$, something its antecedent already entails. Since $p \wedge (p \wedge q)$ is just $p \wedge q$, (RS) is the restriction of Lewis's Factorization principle, $Pr(B > C \mid A) = Pr(C \mid A \wedge B)$, to the case in which $B$ entails $A$. Given Stalnaker's Thesis, unrestricted Factorization is equivalent to Import-Export and leads to triviality (Lewis 1976; Égré and Rott 2021, §5.2). The triviality argument conditions on the consequent. When $Pr(A \wedge C)$ and $Pr(A \wedge \neg C)$ are positive,
$$Pr(A > C) = Pr(A > C \mid C)Pr(C) + Pr(A > C \mid \neg C)Pr(\neg C) = Pr(C \mid A \wedge C)Pr(C) + Pr(C \mid A \wedge \neg C)Pr(\neg C) = Pr(C).$$
(RS) licenses the middle step only if $A$ entails $C$ or entails $\neg C$, and those are exactly the cases excluded by the positivity conditions. So (RS) never licenses it.

(RS) is also satisfiable without triviality. Consider van Fraassen's construction, in which sequences of worlds are drawn independently from a base distribution and a conditional is evaluated at the tail of a sequence that begins with its first antecedent-world. Conditioning on $p$ fixes only that the first world drawn is a $p$-world. If that world is also a $p \wedge q$-world, it settles $r$, and it is an $r$-world with probability $Pr(r \mid p \wedge q)$. If it is not, the first $p \wedge q$-world comes later, is drawn independently of the first, and so is again an $r$-world with probability $Pr(r \mid p \wedge q)$. So (RS) holds whatever the base distribution. I verified in exact rational arithmetic, for randomly chosen base distributions over the valuations of $p$, $q$ and $r$, that (RS) holds in these models. Both sides of Flattening receive probability $Pr(r \mid p \wedge q)$, and unrestricted Factorization fails.

Given the Thesis for the two conditionals involved, then, the probabilistic content of Flattening is (RS): the Thesis is stable under conditioning on what the antecedent entails. Supposition by conditioning makes this natural, because conditioning is cumulative. The result answers Dorr and Mandelkern's second question only at the level of probability. They ask for an argument from a restricted Thesis to the *validity* of a restricted Flattening. Proposition 4 shows what such an argument must exploit. What remains is a bridge from equal probability under every admissible credence function to logical equivalence, and its credentials depend on which credence functions are admissible. I do not settle that here.

Now take the indicative version of the Reunion. You do not know whether Ann or Bob came, and you regard each as unlikely and the two as independent. "If one of them came, then if Ann came, Bob came" and "If Ann came, Bob came" are then credible to the same degree, $Pr(B \mid A) = Pr(B)$, as (RS) predicts. The indicative supposition that one of them came does not commit to Bob. What about the indicative counterpart of (D)? On the Thesis, "if Bob came, Ann didn't" is credible to degree $Pr(\neg A \mid B) = Pr(\neg A)$, the degree to which Ann probably did not come in any case. That is no claim of dependence. The counterfactual (D) is false because it attributes a dependence of each person's coming on the other's absence, which the story rules out. The indicative counterpart attributes nothing of the kind. I do not claim that indicative judgments about such embeddings support Flattening, only that the case against it does not transfer. Whether arguments against Import-Export for indicatives extend to Flattening is a separate question.

## 6. Objections and replies

**6.1 Disjunctive antecedents.** Many hear "had Ann or Bob come" as simplifying to "had Ann come, and had Bob come". On that reading (P2) is false, and so is $(A \vee B) > (A > B)$, since one of the scenarios considered is Ann's. I have four replies. First, the target logic contains C2, which is closed under RCEA. Together with RCEA, the simplification of disjunctive antecedents yields antecedent strengthening (Ellis, Jackson and Pargetter 1977), and together with CEM it has further implausible consequences (Cariani and Goldstein 2020). Proponents of C2.F therefore cannot appeal to it, and Theorem 1 does not use (P2) in any case. Second, selectional readings of disjunctive antecedents are well attested. McKay and van Inwagen's (1977) Spain example is a standard case against the simplification, and experimental work reports support for selectional semantics over universal and homogeneity-based alternatives (Ramotowska et al. 2025). Third, the disjunction can be removed. Let the antecedent be "had someone from their year come", where Ann and Bob are the only two members of their year who might have come. The proof of Theorem 1 goes through unchanged, since it uses only the fact that the outer antecedent's proposition contains $[A]$ and $[B]$ and is contained in their union. Fourth, on a universal reading of disjunctive antecedents Proposition 2(b) still fails. The scenario in which Bob comes is among those considered, and in it Bob would still have come had Ann come. So $(A \vee B) > (A > \neg B)$ is false while $A > \neg B$ is true.

**6.2 Hyperintensional antecedents.** Truthmaker and alternative-sensitive semantics deny that classically equivalent antecedents can be intersubstituted (Fine 2012; Santorio 2018). Ciardelli, Zhang and Champollion (2018) report that antecedents equivalent in truth conditions make different contributions in a two-switch scenario. A theorist of this kind may refuse to replace $(A \vee B) \wedge A$ by $A$. The reply is that C2.F is closed under RCEA, so the argument stands against it. A hyperintensional version of Flattening faces the existential version of the case from 6.1, which has no overt disjunction. The diagnosis of Section 4 also does not depend on how antecedents are individuated. Whenever the scenario selected for a weaker antecedent realizes it in a way that a stronger supposition made from inside the scenario keeps, and a direct supposition does not, Flattening fails. However antecedents are individuated, "had someone from their year come" is realized by some scenario, and "had Ann come", supposed from inside a scenario in which Bob came, keeps Bob there.

**6.3 Embedded counterfactuals evaluated from actuality.** An objector might hold that a counterfactual embedded under another is evaluated by the actual world's standards, not those of the scenario the outer antecedent introduces. Then "had Ann come, Bob would not have" keeps its actual truth value inside any supposition, (D) comes out true, and its apparent falsity is a confusion. The view proves too much. It makes outer antecedents idle on embedded counterfactuals: whenever $p$ is possible, $p > (q > r)$ would be true iff $q > r$ is. But "had Bob come, then had Ann come too, Bob would have come" is plainly true, and "had Ann come, Bob would have come" is plainly false. Embedded counterfactuals are evaluated from the supposed scenario. That is what makes the first sentence true, and it is all the argument of Section 3 needs.

**6.4 Skepticism about counterfactual independence.** Goodsell (2022), discussing Morgenbesser's coin, argues that the common disposition to infer counterfactual independence from causal independence is mistaken. Skeptics about counterfactuals also deny "would" counterfactuals when chance intervenes between antecedent and consequent (see Starr and Kocurek 2025, §7.1). In C2, (N1) is equivalent to $A > \neg B$ when $A$ is possible. So a C2 theorist moved by such considerations might deny that (N1) is true, on the ground that whether Bob's babysitter cancels is chancy and a world in which Ann flew in would have diverged weeks earlier. The argument does not need cases of that kind.

> *The Buttons.* At noon, Ann in Sydney and Bob in London could each have pressed a button. Each hesitated at the last instant and did not. The world is deterministic, and nothing that happened in either city in the final instant before noon could affect the other city at noon, since no signal could cover the distance in time.

A counterfactual departure confined to Ann's last instant before noon leaves London at noon as it actually was, and likewise with the cities reversed. So (N1) and (N2) hold. (D) is false: in the scenario in which one of them presses, the other's pressing would have made no difference to whether the first pressed. This holds even on a miracle-based similarity theory of the kind Lewis (1979) proposed, since perfect match with actuality is preserved everywhere outside the future of a small, late departure. Whatever the merits of skeptical arguments for cases like Morgenbesser's, where a chancy outcome follows the antecedent, the Buttons involve no chance and require no inference from causal independence.

**6.5 This is just similarity again.** One might suspect that the argument merely restates the fact that similarity semantics fails to validate Flattening. It does not. Theorem 1 is a theorem of C2.F, and its premises are judgments about a case, not assumptions of a semantics. Proposition 2 holds in every normal conditional logic. The case shows that counterfactuals lack the property that similarity lacks. Nor does the argument depend on hard-to-assess embedded sentences: Section 4 restates it as a contrast between successive and direct suppositions.

**6.6 C2.F was meant for indicatives.** If so, the results support that restriction and explain it. But C2 is Stalnaker's logic of conditionals, counterfactual ones included (Stalnaker 1968), and Dorr and Mandelkern (2026) find all of C2's principles plausible for natural-language conditionals. A reader could therefore have taken C2.F to be a candidate for counterfactuals as well. The results rule that out. Neither C2.F nor C2.FS is a logic of counterfactuals, and sequence semantics is not a semantics for them.

## 7. Conclusion

I have shown four things. In C2.F, two negative counterfactual premises, (N1) and (N2), entail a claim of counterfactual dependence, (D), which is false in the Reunion and in the Buttons. Either direction of Flattening fails for counterfactuals in any normal conditional logic. Flattening is valid on a selection frame exactly when imaging on that frame is cumulative. And given Stalnaker's Thesis, the probabilistic content of Flattening is a restricted Factorization principle that is satisfiable without triviality.

I have not shown that Flattening is valid for indicatives, nor derived its validity from any probabilistic principle. What follows is that Flattening, the weakest fragment of Import-Export, already separates indicative from counterfactual conditionals. The line it draws runs between supposition made from an information state and supposition made from a world. For proponents of C2.F, the open problem becomes sharper. They must show that indicative supposition, including supposition under embedding, is cumulative in the sense of Theorem 3, for instance by an account on which selection for indicative conditionals represents conditioning rather than similarity.
