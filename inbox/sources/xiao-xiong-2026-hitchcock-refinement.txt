ARTICLE
Ar e ﬁnement of the Hitchcock de ﬁnition of actual
causation
Zhizun Xiao 1 & Wei Xiong 1 ✉
Models of actual causation seek to capture the causal relationships between two speci ﬁc
events within a given context, thereby contributing to a clear understanding of causality. A
key challenge in constructing such models lies in providing a rigorous and well-de ﬁned notion
of actual causation. Several in ﬂuential approaches have been proposed in the literature,
including the Hitchcock de ﬁnition, the modi ﬁed Halpern-Pearl de ﬁnition, and the Halpern-
Hitchcock deﬁnition. However, these de ﬁnitions struggle to offer a uni ﬁed solution to certain
canonical problems, such as the switch example, the short circuit example, the bogus pre-
vention example, and the switch-variant example. To address this limitation, we propose a
deﬁnition for actual causation by formalizing and re ﬁning the Hitchcock de ﬁnition. This
reﬁned de ﬁnition offers a framework capable of resolving these canonical problems. Addi-
tionally, we present an analysis comparing these de ﬁnitions, demonstrating that our proposed
deﬁnition is more restrictive than both the Hitchcock and modi ﬁed Halpern-Pearl de ﬁnitions.

Introduction
C
ausation is a fundamental concept that plays a critical role
across a wide range of disciplines, including arti ﬁcial
intelligence, philosophy, law, biomedicine, economics, and
epidemiology, among others. Its importance has inspired exten-
sive interdisciplinary research, as scholars seek to explore its
implications and applications.
Within the literature, causation is generally divided into two
main categories: type causation (general causation) and actual
causation (token causation). Type causation refers to a relation-
ship between types of events in general contexts, whereas actual
causation describes a relationship between speci ﬁc, concrete
events within a particular scenario — events tied to particular
individuals, places, or times (Hitchcock ( 2018)).1 For example,
the statement “smoking causes lung cancer ” represents type
causation, as it captures a general causal relationship. In contrast,
the statement “Lucy’s smoking ten cigarettes daily for twenty
years caused her to develop lung cancer this year ” illustrates
token causation, as it refers to a speci ﬁc causal instance involving
a particular individual and a de ﬁnite time frame. Beyond such
speciﬁc instances, theories of actual causation are crucial. They
not only deepen our understanding of the nature of causality but
also provide a theoretical framework for addressing practical
societal issues, such as responsibility attribution and blame
assignment.
To effectively model actual causation, it is crucial to formulate a
general deﬁnition. In recent years, substantial research efforts have
been devoted to addressing this challenge (see, e.g., Beckers (2021);
Beckers and Vennekens ( 2018); Bochman ( 2018); Hall ( 2007);
Halpern ( 2008, 2015); Halpern and Hitchcock ( 2015); Hitchcock
(2001); Menzies (2007); Pearl (2000); Weslake (2015); Zhu (2022)).
Most de ﬁnitions of actual causation are rooted in the counter-
factual approach introduced by Lewis ( 1973a), which posits that C
is a cause of E if, in the absence of C, E would not have occurred. To
resolve the issue of early preemption, Lewis deﬁned causation as the
transitive closure of causal dependence among actual events (Lewis
(1979)).2 However, this approach entails the transitivity of causa-
tion as a corollary, which has been challenged in the literature on
the basis of various counterexamples (Hall ( 2000)).
One approach to identifying a causal relation without relying
on the transitivity principle is to hold certain variables ﬁxed at
their actual values, thereby revealing hidden counterfactual
dependencies (Hitchcock ( 2001)). Hitchcock employs explicitly
nonforetracking counterfactuals to de ﬁne an active route between
two events. The core idea is to identify an active route between
the putative cause and the effect, such that altering the value of
the putative cause along this route would also change the value of
the effect variable. This approach, referred to as the Hitchcock
(HIT) de ﬁnition, aligns naturally with our intuitive causal judg-
ments by appealing to psychologically natural counterfactuals
rather than philosophically technical ones (Hitchcock ( 2001)).
However, the HIT de ﬁnition struggles to satisfactorily resolve
several canonical examples, such as the switch and short circuit
problems (Hitchcock ( 2001)). A similar idea of ﬁxing variables at
their actual values is also central to the modi ﬁed Halpern-Pearl
(MHP) de ﬁnition (Halpern ( 2015)), which is widely regarded as
an in ﬂuential de ﬁnition of actual causation in the current litera-
ture. Despite its formal elegance, the MHP de ﬁnition similarly
fails to resolve these problems.
A prominent alternative approach involves incorporating
notions of normality into accounts of actual causation (Hall
(2007); Halpern ( 2008); Halpern and Hitchcock ( 2015); Hitch-
cock ( 2007); Icard et al. ( 2017); Menzies ( 2004, 2007)). This
approach is supported by empirical research indicating that
people’s judgments of actual causation are in ﬂuenced by their
perceptions of the normality of events (see Hitchcock and Knobe
(2009)). For instance, Halpern and Hitchcock ( 2015) introduce a
normality ordering over possible worlds to rank causes based on
their most normal witness. The Halpern and Hitchcock (HH)
deﬁnition, which builds on this idea, successfully addresses the
short circuit example (Halpern and Hitchcock ( 2015)). The
authors emphasize that the structural equations within the model
play a crucial role in determining the normality ordering. Simi-
larly, Weslake ( 2015) proposes a related approach to handle the
switch example, suggesting that the relevant possibilities within
the model should be exhaustive. In his framework, there are
typically only two possibilities, both leading to the same outcome.
Introducing a third possibility that yields a different outcome
could lead to confusion and unsatisfactory results. These cases
highlight a subtle yet signi ﬁcant constraint that profoundly
inﬂuences our causal judgments.
Inspired by these insights, we re ﬁne the HIT de ﬁnition by
introducing a condition on the notion of active routes and for-
malizing it within the structural equation framework of Halpern
and Pearl (Halpern and Pearl ( 2005)). Speci ﬁcally, we consider
whether the possible worlds resulting from a hypothetical inter-
vention are consistent with the given context. This re ﬁnement
yields a relatively simple and elegant de ﬁnition that provides a
uniﬁed solution to several canonical problems in the literature.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section“Basic
concepts” introduces the formal framework of structural equations
and reviews the HIT, MHP, and HH deﬁnitions. Section“AP r o b l e m
in the HIT and MHP de ﬁnitions” discusses a key problem faced by
both the HIT and MHP de ﬁnitions. In Section “Ar e ﬁned HIT
deﬁnition”,w er e ﬁne the HIT de ﬁnition by incorporating a condi-
tion on the notion of active routes and establish a theoretical result
regarding the relationships between these de ﬁnitions. Section
“Analyzing other examples” applies the new de ﬁnition to several
canonical examples, comparing its performance with other de ﬁni-
tions. Finally, Section “Concluding Remarks” concludes the paper.

Three de ﬁnitions of actual causation . Inspired by Halpern and
Pearl ( 2005), Hitchcock presents structural equations as tools for
representing patterns of counterfactual dependence. He uses
explicitly nonforetracking counterfactuals to de ﬁne the notion of
an active route between two events (Hitchcock ( 2001)). Through
this route, the value of the putative cause variable determines the
value of the effect variable, indicating a counterfactual depen-
dence between them. If there are multiple routes between the
putative cause and the effect, variables on other routes need to be
ﬁxed at their actual values. With this idea, actual causation can be
identiﬁed by ﬁnding an active route on the diagram.

ðS; FÞ if and only if Z depends counterfactually upon X within a

Y2 U ∪ V,i f Y is intermediate between X and Z but does not

for Y with a new equation that sets Y equal to its actual value in
F .
Note that if there is no intermediate variable that does not
belong to this route, then F0 is the same as F .
Deﬁnition 7 . (The HIT de ﬁnition) Let c and e be distinct
occurrent events, and let X and Z be variables such that the values
of X and Z represent alterations of c and e, respectively. Then c is
a cause of e if and only if there is an active causal route from X to
Z in an appropriate causal model ðS; FÞ.
Halpern ( 2015) offers a de ﬁnition for actual causation that is
elegant and provides a good reference for formalizing our
deﬁnition. The key idea of this de ﬁnition is that keeping some
variables ﬁxed at their actual values uncovers hidden counter-
factual dependence.

A problem in the HIT and MHP de ﬁnitions
Although the HIT and MHP de ﬁnitions can effectively address
the problem of early preemption without invoking the transitivity
principle, they fail to adequately explain some canonical exam-
ples, such as the switch example (Hall ( 2007)).
Example 1 . (Switch) An engineer is standing by a switch in the
railroad tracks. A train approaches in the distance. She ﬂips the
switch, causing the train to travel down the right-hand track
instead of the left. Since the tracks reconverge ahead, the train
arrives at its destination regardless. Is the engineer ’s ﬂipping of
the switch a cause of the train ’s arrival?
Figure 2 illustrates the causal structure of the example, where
EF stands for the engineer ﬂipping the switch; TR denotes the
train traveling on the right-hand track; TL denotes the train
traveling on the left-hand track; and TA represents the train ’s
arrival.

TL = 0, TA = 1). We now analyze the example using the HIT
deﬁnition. From Fig. 2, there are two paths from EF to TA: P1 =
(EF, TR, TA), P2 = (EF, TL, TA). Considering P1,w e ﬁx TL at its

indicates that TA counterfactually depends on EF, implying an
active route ( EF, TR, TA) from EF to TA. According to the HIT
deﬁnition, EF = 1 is an actual cause of TA = 1, which contradicts
our intuition.
Next, we analyze the example using the MHP de ﬁnition.
Clearly, AC1 and AC3 hold. We take ~W¼f TLg and w = 0; that
is, we hold TL at its actual value of 0. If we set EF = 0, then TA =
0, showing that AC2( am) holds. Therefore, we also conclude that
EF = 1 is an actual cause of TA = 1.
However, our intuition suggests that the engineer ’s ﬂipping of
the switch is not an actual cause of the train ’s arrival. This is
because ﬂipping the switch does not actually change the outcome.
If the engineer had not ﬂipped the switch, the train would still
have arrived at its destination.
One popular approach to addressing this problem is to
incorporate the notion of normality into accounts of actual
causation. Normality can typically be divided into two types:
descriptive (statistical) norms and prescriptive norms (Hitchcock
and Knobe ( 2009)). Descriptive norms are grounded in statistical
tendencies, such as the statistical mode or mean, whereas pre-
scriptive norms are rooted in how things ought to be or are
supposed to be, encompassing moral, legal, and other normative
standards. These norms collectively guide judgments of what
constitutes appropriate behavior in a given situation.
The HH de ﬁnition provides a ﬂexible formal framework for
incorporating normality into the account of actual causation. It
uses a normality ordering on the set of possible worlds to rank
causes according to their most normal witness. Although the HH
deﬁnition is powerful and can address many problems, it still has
limitations, which will be discussed in the next section. Addi-
tionally, con ﬂicts may emerge between descriptive and pre-
scriptive norms — an issue even acknowledged by Halpern and
Hitchcock themselves (Halpern and Hitchcock ( 2015)).
Ar e ﬁned HIT de ﬁnition
In this section, we address the aforementioned problems in the
HIT and HH de ﬁnitions by re ﬁning the HIT de ﬁnition through
the addition of a condition on the notion of active routes. Fur-
thermore, we establish a result illustrating the relationships
between these de ﬁnitions.
Although the HH de ﬁnition is powerful, it encounters dif ﬁ-
culties in the switch-variant example , where both tracks are
blocked (Halpern ( 2016), p.90).

deﬁnition is overly permissive, as it allows variables outside the
causal pathway to be ﬁxed at non-actual values. By considering a
broader set of counterfactual worlds, this approach increases the
likelihood of classifying an event as a cause. Although the HH
deﬁnition incorporates normality constraints to restrict con-

example, the witness world ( EF = 0, LB = 0, RB = 1, TA = 1) is

inappropriately identiﬁes the engineer ’s ﬂipping of the switch as a
cause.
Deﬁnition 12 . (Availability) A world is said to be available in a
causal model if it does not violate the structural equations of
the model.
Essentially, an available world in a causal model is one that is
consistent with the given scenario. For example, in the switch
example, there are 16 possible worlds, as there are four elements
in the set of endogenous variables. The structural equations in
this example are de ﬁned as TR = EF, TL = (1 − TR), TA =
max{TL, TR}. According to the scenario, if EF = 1, then TR = 1,
TL = 0, and TA = 1. Conversely, if EF = 0, then TR = 0, TL = 1,
and TA = 1. Therefore, the world ( EF = 1, TR = 1, TL = 0, TA =
1) and ( EF = 0, TR = 0, TL = 1, TA = 1) are available in the
model MS. In contrast, the other worlds are not available in the
model, because they violate the structural equations.
As previously established, ﬂipping the switch does not qualify
as a cause of the train ’s arrival in the standard switch example.
However, it would be deemed a cause if we account for the
possibility that the tracks might be blocked. Thus, when the
speciﬁc details of a scenario change, the corresponding models
and conclusions shift accordingly. From this perspective, the
structural equations that constrain the notion of availability are
contingent. Nevertheless, studies of actual causation focus on
exploring causal relationships within a speci ﬁc scenario, where
the causal in ﬂuences between events are modeled through a set of
structural equations. Once a speci ﬁc narrative context is provided
and background information is fully supplemented, a causal
model can be derived; in this case, the structural equations are
also determined.
We return to the HIT de ﬁnition, which is more natural and
only ﬁxes variables at their actual values. In the switch example,
Weslake ( 2015) notes that the relevant possibilities should be
exhaustive. As we can see, there are only two available worlds in
this example: ( EF = 1, TR = 1, TL = 0, TA = 1) and ( EF = 0, TR
= 0, TL = 1, TA = 1), both of which yield the same result. It is
therefore reasonable to conclude that EF = 1 is not an actual
cause of TA = 1. If the world ( EF = 0, TR = 0, TL = 0, TA = 0)
were available, ﬂipping the switch could be considered as a cause
— but this is not the case in the switch example. Evidently, the
notion of availability helps address the switch example.
The idea that possible worlds should be available in a causal
model can also address the short circuit example (Hitchcock
(2007)). In this example, there are two possible worlds, both
yielding the same result. Consequently, nothing is considered a
cause (more details about this example will be discussed in Sec-
tion 5). Beckers and Vennekens ( 2018) argue that this example is
essentially no different from the switch example. Halpern and
Hitchcock ( 2015) contend that the structural equations in the
model signi ﬁcantly in ﬂuence the normality ordering. Halpern
(2015) further discusses that the witness is an abnormal world
that contradicts the story.

Concluding remarks
Providing a precise de ﬁnition of causation is a fundamental step
in constructing models for actual causation. The HIT, MHP, and
HH de ﬁnitions offer valuable frameworks for determining causal
relationships between speci ﬁc events. However, because the
possible worlds resulting from a hypothetical intervention may be
inconsistent with the given context, these de ﬁnitions sometimes
produce unsatisfactory causal judgments in certain situations.
Consequently, they fail to provide a uniform solution to some
canonical problems, such as the switch example, the short circuit
example, the bogus prevention example, and the switch-variant
example.
To address this problem in these causal theories, we propose a
reﬁned de ﬁnition of actual causation by formalizing and re ﬁning
the HIT deﬁnition. This reﬁned deﬁnition provides a treatment of
all the aforementioned canonical examples. Our key insight is
that certain subtle restrictions, which signi ﬁcantly in ﬂuence
causal judgments, must be incorporated into the modeling of
actual causation. Speci ﬁcally, it is essential to determine whether
the possible worlds generated by hypothetical interventions are
consistent with a given causal context. By integrating this
restriction into the causal models, we propose an alternative
approach to determining causal relationships.
To be sure, this restriction might give rise to undesired con-
sequences. For instance, the RHIT de ﬁnition entails that the
trainee’s ﬁring does not count as a cause of the victim ’s death in
the early preemption scenario. Nevertheless, we can resolve this
counterintuitive conclusion by drawing on Beckers and Venne-
kens’ analysis of the early preemption example (Beckers and
Vennekens ( 2017, 2018)). In their work, they argue that the key
distinction between the early preemption and the switch example
lies in whether we take into account the possibility that the
backup mechanism malfunctions. More speci ﬁcally, since it is
quite plausible to regard the supervisor ’s shooting accuracy as far
more uncertain than the breakage of a sturdy track, it is
reasonable to expect that intuitions supporting the trainee ’s ﬁring
as a cause are more prevalent than those endorsing the ﬂip of the
switch as a cause. Hence, an appropriate model for the early
preemption scenario should incorporate a variable representing
the uncertainty inherent in the counterfactual narrative, which
stands in contrast to the model constructed for the switch
example (Beckers and Vennekens ( 2018)).
From this perspective, a proper formal model of the early
preemption scenario should be nondeterministic — reasonably
allowing for the possibility that even the supervisor may not
always ﬁre accurately or might similarly lose his nerve. This idea
supports the view that our intuitive judgments do not treat the
early preemption example as a deterministic case, and that the
causal model should accordingly re ﬂect this nondeterminism. To
capture the supervisor ’s potential inaccuracy or hesitation, we can
introduce additional variables such as Accurate (representing
whether the supervisor ’s ﬁring hits the target) and Nerve
(representing whether the supervisor maintains the resolve to
ﬁre). Integrating these variables into the model yields a more
adequate, nondeterministic formalization, which allows us to
conclude that the trainee ’s ﬁring is a cause of the victim ’s death.
An alternative approach to representing the restriction in causal
models can be based on similarity semantics. Lewis ( 1973b) pro-
posed a method for evaluating counterfactuals that incorporates
the notion of comparative similarity: one orders possible worlds
according to their distance from the actual world, and the coun-
terfactual “If it were A, then B” is declared true in a world w if and
only if B is true in all the closest A-worlds to w. Lewis ( 1979)
subsequently established an intricate system of weights and prio-
rities for various aspects of similarity, aiming to align similarity
more closely with causal intuitions. It is for this reason that the
framework of similarity semantics offers an alternative approach
to interpreting the speci ﬁc meaning of normality. Furthermore,
similarity semantics may provide a feasible method for re ﬁning
the conditions in the deﬁnition of availability. Drawing on insights
from this semantics to establish a set of normative assumptions
that select a witness world yielding the desired conclusion thus
constitutes a promising avenue for future research — one that we
intend to explore in our subsequent work.
